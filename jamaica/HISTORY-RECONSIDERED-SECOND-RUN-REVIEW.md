## The Taíno story does not end neatly with “extinction.”
Archaeology, living heritage and genetic research ask different questions. Treating them as one verdict obscures both colonial catastrophe and the limits of what we know about Jamaica.

### The account under examination
A people declared extinct have no further place in Jamaican history beyond a few borrowed words.

What the evidence changes: Catastrophic conquest, surviving cultural practices, genetic ancestry and present-day Indigenous identity are different historical subjects. Evidence for one cannot automatically establish—or erase—the others.

### Start with a settlement, not a disappearance
White Marl in St. Catherine offers a better beginning than an extinction date. Siegel, Beier and Kaiman describe a major pre-colonial settlement whose meaning extends beyond excavated objects to residents, heritage managers and Indigenous descendants. Their study examines the relationships between archaeological interpretation and living claims to the place. It is a study of heritage and continuity, not a genetic census of Jamaica. [1]

An inhabited landscape changes the questions we ask. Who selected a settlement location? How did households obtain food? What skills made a carved object or a pot possible? An opening sentence about disappearance can make all this social history feel like a brief prelude to the supposedly real history that arrives by European ship. That is a choice of narrative, not a requirement imposed by the evidence.

### A useful name can hide differences
The Smithsonian’s account of a Lucayan duho, or carved seat, warns that the label Taíno can obscure cultural diversity across the Greater Antilles. Its featured object comes from the Turks and Caicos/Bahamas region, not Jamaica. Shared terminology helps comparison, but does not turn every island into one interchangeable society. [2]

The same care belongs in our pictures. A magnificent object from another island can illuminate a regional tradition when its actual origin remains visible. Relabelling it as Jamaican would make the page more dramatic while making the history worse. Regional resemblance is evidence to investigate, not permission to transfer an object’s provenance.

### What DNA answers—and what it does not
Schroeder and colleagues sequenced an ancient individual from Preacher’s Cave in the Bahamas and identified relationships between that genome and Indigenous ancestry in some present-day Caribbean populations. This challenges a blanket regional claim that every biological lineage vanished. The ancient person was not excavated in Jamaica, and the findings do not establish the ancestry of every Jamaican or a particular Jamaican family. [3]

Genetic relationship is not the same as an unbroken language, a continuously recognized political community or a documented family tree. Conversely, a community’s heritage cannot be dismissed merely because no genetic study was found for its members. Each proposition needs evidence suited to it. A commercial ancestry category alone cannot resolve a specific seventeenth-century Jamaican relationship.

### Keep catastrophe visible
Kenneth Morgan’s chapter on Jamaican Taíno history foregrounds settlements, craft, cultivation and religious practice, and notes a renewed public interest in Indigenous history. Its description of the surviving evidence differs from the DNA study’s research problem. Archaeology and historical synthesis recover ways of living; neither should be presented as a complete record of every person after conquest. [4]

Questioning an absolute extinction narrative does not reduce the violence of colonization. Nor does an assertion of survival establish that social life continued without rupture. The responsible reconsideration holds loss, transformation and the limits of surviving records together. It refuses the false choice between total disappearance and an unchanged people waiting intact to be rediscovered.

### Visit with a better question
At Seville, use the guide’s archaeological history to connect Indigenous occupation with subsequent colonial landscapes. At White Marl, begin with the published heritage study rather than assuming an excavated site is an empty space available for tourism. A scholarly discussion of a place does not establish current visitor access or permission to enter it.

The question worth carrying is specific: what form of continuity is being claimed, and what evidence establishes it? A crop, a remembered place, a named descendant community and a DNA result may all matter. They do not all prove the same thing. That distinction gives Indigenous history a serious place in Jamaica’s present without inventing certainty.

## Tacky’s revolt was a war with more than one front.
Vincent Brown’s interpretation moves beyond one leader and one parish to examine African politics, Jamaican terrain and imperial conflict together.

### The account under examination
Tacky’s death ended an isolated St. Mary disturbance by people reacting without a wider political world.

What the evidence changes: The 1760–61 conflicts require an islandwide and Atlantic frame. Brown’s interpretation restores military knowledge and competing political projects without claiming that every uprising followed one proven master plan.

### A name is not the boundary of a conflict
Tacky’s name supplies a memorable point of entry. It cannot by itself define the duration, geography or participants of the conflicts of 1760–61. The guide’s local account begins at Fort Haldane and St. Mary; this essay asks what changes when we follow the war beyond that opening. Ending the account with one leader would make later action difficult to see.

Brown’s book, presented through a substantial excerpt by the Museum of the American Revolution, places the conflict within African warfare, slavery, struggles among Black communities and the Seven Years’ War. This is his interpretive framework, not a declaration that four neatly separated armies fought four separate campaigns. [1]

### Africa was a political world
The museum’s excerpt explains why Coromantee should not be treated as a timeless, homogeneous tribe. Shared languages and practices could become resources for building new affiliations after displacement. Enslavers’ classifications were not transparent descriptions of how captives understood themselves. [1]

That distinction matters when a popular account makes rebelliousness an inherited racial trait. Organization has a history: people learn, form alliances, communicate and respond to circumstances. Restoring political agency does not require portraying every participant as equally trained or giving every person the same objective. It requires abandoning a vocabulary that makes planning disappear behind supposed instinct.

### Terrain changes the account
Axis Maps’ project for Brown charts events, approximate routes and clashes across historic basemaps. Its documentation explicitly acknowledges that the narrative database is not exhaustive and historical data raise reliability problems. The visual reconstruction gives us a way to inspect movement; it does not create precision that the sources lack. [2]

A map asks useful questions that a hero portrait cannot answer. What was the relationship between a plantation, a road and an interior refuge? How far apart were reported actions? A route drawn between points is an interpretation to examine, especially when marked approximate. An attractive line should never become an eyewitness statement merely because a screen displays it confidently.

### Freedom was not one identical programme
The first run’s Maroon essay explains the treaty obligations that could place free Maroon communities against later fugitives and rebels. Read it alongside this piece. Shared African descent did not erase differences in legal status, security or local political interests. The reminder complicates the history; it does not supply a blanket moral verdict on every Maroon person.

It is equally risky to assign the rebels a fully formed twentieth-century nationalism. Resistance to enslavement, control of territory and the imagined government of a new community are related questions, not synonyms. Brown’s framework invites attention to multiple conflicts and loyalties. Where a participant’s intentions are not recoverable, we should leave the uncertainty in view.

## 1938 was made by workers. Women belong in the account.
Party leaders, wage records, oral testimony and Sistren’s theatre offer different views of the upheaval. Recovering women requires more than adding a heroine to the familiar political story.

### The account under examination
The labour rebellion can be adequately explained through the biographies of its best-known male political leaders.

What the evidence changes: Leadership matters, but the movement’s social history also requires workers, households and women’s experiences. A later performance can challenge public memory without proving every incident it dramatizes.

### Begin before the platform speech
Colin Palmer’s Freedom’s Children connects the labour rebellion and its aftermath to the emergence of Bustamante and Manley and to constitutional and party politics. The publisher’s account also begins with workers challenging colonial conditions. Moving immediately to the two leaders would remove the people whose grievances made political leadership consequential. [1]

A leader’s speech and a worker’s decision to join a protest are different historical actions. So are keeping a household fed, losing wages and deciding whether the risks of confrontation are bearable. These are questions for the records, not an invitation to invent an unnamed woman’s thoughts. A fuller account must seek the social conditions that a political biography can leave outside its frame.

### Oral history changes who can enter
UWI’s Social History Project identifies Not for Wages Alone, edited by Patrick Bryan and Karl Watson, as a collection based on 35 oral testimonies about the conditions surrounding 1938. It also lists Linnette Vassell’s Voices of Women in Jamaica, 1898–1939. These are distinct research resources; their existence does not mean their full contents are openly available online. [2]

A remembered event should be assessed through the speaker’s position, the interval before the interview and comparison with other evidence. Those questions strengthen oral history rather than disqualifying it. Official dispatches also have authors, audiences and purposes. Treating a governor’s report as neutral while dismissing a worker’s recollection as merely subjective would impose an unequal evidentiary standard.

### A woman at the centre of a later retelling
Karina Smith analyzes Sistren’s 1985 production Ida Revolt inna Jonkonnu Stylee. The repository abstract describes its woman-centred retelling and identifies the story that Ida Homes threw an initiating stone as a rumour. That qualification must survive our retelling. The play is evidence of how the past was reworked in 1985, not independent proof of the alleged stone-throwing. [3]

There is a valuable surprise here. Correcting an exclusion does not require certifying a dramatic origin story. We can examine why the production put a woman at its centre, what theatrical traditions it used and which questions it raised. That makes the performance historically meaningful without changing its evidentiary category to satisfy our desire for a tidy beginning.

### The official account already framed the dispute
On 4 May 1938, Colonial Secretary William Ormsby-Gore relayed the governor’s account of Frome to Parliament: a wage dispute during factory construction, a strike for higher wages, and police firing on 2 May. He reported four deaths, nine wounded people and 93 arrests, while presenting the disturbances as local to the estate. These are the figures and characterization in that contemporary official report, not a final independently reconstructed casualty count. [4]

The report names official actions and totals; it does not identify each woman’s experience. That gap explains why questions about households and participation require other sources. An official summary can be indispensable evidence while remaining insufficient for the social history of the event.

### Work is larger than the wage packet
The Frome article supplies a documented local sequence and an official report to inspect. Follow the dispute there before generalizing it into the entire island’s rebellion. A photograph of the completed factory belongs to industrial history; it should not be labelled as the confrontation during construction. A learning chronology should likewise remain labelled as an editorial graphic.

To investigate women’s participation seriously, compare named workers, occupations, household circumstances and the political language used about them. Ask which forms of labour the source counts and which it overlooks. A claim about unpaid household work should be grounded in evidence about actual households, not inserted as a universal explanation that bypasses local differences.

### Restore the people without erasing leadership
The point is not to replace two famous men with one newly celebrated woman. It is to change the structure of the account so collective action, social conditions and subsequent memory can all be examined. Party organization and constitutional change remain consequential; they become more intelligible when we recover the population to whom politics had to answer.

Our conclusion is deliberately bounded. The sources reviewed establish a serious scholarly and documentary route into workers’ and women’s histories, and a later theatrical challenge to public memory. They do not supply a verified roster of every woman present in every protest. That roster’s absence from this review is a research limit, not proof that women were absent.

## Obeah: the accusation has a history too.
Court categories and colonial ideas shaped what counted as Obeah. Diana Paton’s research asks us to investigate the label as well as the people placed beneath it.

### The account under examination
Obeah is a single, self-evident practice whose historical meaning can be read directly from a prosecution or a frightening story.

What the evidence changes: The term’s meanings and uses changed. Studying spiritual practice, an allegation and a legal conviction requires separate questions; historical explanation neither endorses supernatural claims nor treats colonial categories as neutral.

### The word can decide the story before it begins
Diana Paton’s The Cultural Politics of Obeah investigates the development and enforcement of anti-Obeah legislation across two centuries. Its publisher description emphasizes how political circumstances shaped interpretations of Obeah. That framing directs our attention to who applied the word, for what purpose and with which consequences. [1]

A report that someone was convicted establishes a legal event. It does not by itself establish that the official description fully captured what the accused believed or did. An allegation of poisoning, a healing practice and a claim to spiritual power must not be quietly merged. The historical category is part of what needs explaining.

### Money became evidence of a particular kind
In her 2009 article, Paton examines a Kingston prosecution reported in 1909. A client working with police paid William Augustus Bruce with marked coins; the coins became material evidence at his trial. Her analysis shows how enforcement focused on payment and constructed the practitioner as a fraud and the client as a dupe. [2]

The case is useful precisely because it is specific. The presence of a payment tells us something about a transaction and the police operation. It does not grant access to every participant’s private beliefs. Nor does one prosecution settle the history of every healer. An account that retains the marked coins, the informant and the courtroom can expose the machinery of accusation without claiming knowledge it does not possess.

### Classification was not socially innocent
Paton notes the contrast between payments for practices prosecuted as Obeah and money collected in Christian services. Her argument concerns the unequal recognition of spiritual authority and the assumptions embedded in legal practice. The comparison asks why certain activities were classified as religion and others as deception. [2]

That is not the claim that all religious transactions are identical or that every accused practitioner acted honestly. Fraud and harm can be investigated as specific conduct. The historical question is whether a category treated one population’s claims to authority as presumptively illegitimate while allowing another’s to operate under different assumptions.

### Neither horror story nor automatic vindication
Obeah and Other Powers, edited by Paton and Maarit Forde, brings together research on religion, healing and political authority. Its contents include Kenneth Bilby on representations in song and Paton on policing and ethnography. It supplies a route into multiple approaches rather than a single dictionary definition that makes the subject disappear. [3]

There is no need to verify a supernatural proposition to take people’s historical circumstances seriously. Likewise, demonstrating colonial repression does not verify a supernatural proposition. Our essay examines records, descriptions and political meanings. It does not advise readers on treatment or turn a historical discussion into instructions for practising a ritual.

### Read an accusation in layers
When encountering a newspaper account, record the alleged act, the offence charged, the evidence described and the outcome separately. Ask whether the reporter observed the practice or repeated police testimony. Distinguish the accused person’s words from the magistrate’s explanation. These are reading questions developed for this guide, not quotations from a historical trial.

The reconsideration is therefore more precise than either dismissing the whole subject as backwardness or celebrating everything beneath the label as resistance. The accusation itself has a history. Following its authors, legal setting and evidentiary structure helps recover people whom sensational language can otherwise render invisible.

## Black Jamaicans helped make Christianity their own.
George Liele, Moses Baker and Black congregations complicate a story in which Europeans supplied religion and Jamaicans merely received it.

### The account under examination
Jamaican Christianity can be explained entirely as a European religion imposed on passive African recipients.

What the evidence changes: Colonial church authority and Black religious leadership existed in the same unequal world. Liele’s and Baker’s ministries show agency and transatlantic connections that a simple one-way transfer leaves out.

### Separate the establishment from the congregation
The guide’s church history examines Anglican institutions within colonial taxation and government. That history matters. It cannot stand in for every denomination, preacher or congregation. The property of an institution and the ideas of a worshipper are related evidence, but neither can automatically substitute for the other.

Noel Leo Erskine’s research on George Liele identifies him as a pastor and educator in Kingston and Spanish Town and traces Moses Baker’s Baptist work in western Jamaica. It also connects Liele’s influence to David George and communities beyond the island. Those networks make Black people organizers and transmitters of religion rather than merely its audience. [1]

### A journey carried obligations as well as belief
The Boston University biography describes Liele’s passage to Jamaica through a loan and indentured service, his arrival in 1783 and his later self-supporting ministry. His movements followed the upheaval of the American Revolution. Religious history here is inseparable from mobility, work and the practical problem of reaching another colony. [2]

The story changes when we begin with the person undertaking that journey. A church did not simply arrive as an abstract doctrine. Someone had to pay passage, find work, obtain permission and form relationships. A biography can recover those actions without suggesting that freedom from personal enslavement removed the constraints of a slave society.

### An African American preacher complicates an imperial origin
Liele’s work preceded the better-known overseas missionary journey of William Carey. The Boston University account uses that chronology to challenge a conventional Baptist missionary starting point. We do not need to make Liele the exclusive origin of all Jamaican Christianity to recognize the importance of this correction. [2]

Chronology matters, but so does the category being compared. The first ordained person, first congregation and first overseas missionary are different claims. A national or denominational celebration can become inaccurate when it swaps one of these achievements for another. Specific credit is stronger than a sweeping superlative that the sources cannot establish.

### Transformation is not simple acceptance
The religious history considered here asks us to distinguish doctrine, institutional allegiance and the lived interpretations of congregations. Being recorded as Baptist does not settle how a person understood spiritual power, authority or freedom. Nor does a European connection make a Jamaican congregation incapable of making its own choices.

This is an interpretation of why leadership and institutions should be studied separately, not a claim to have recovered every congregation’s beliefs. For such recovery, letters, covenants, preaching accounts and testimony would need to be compared. The Boston University bibliography identifies Liele’s 1793 account as a primary source; it is a next step in the evidence, not a document silently claimed as fully read here. [2]

### Avoid replacing one absolute with another
Black leadership does not acquit every church of complicity with slavery. Colonial entanglement does not erase every Black religious project. These conclusions can coexist because they concern different actors and structures. The historical work becomes more demanding when we abandon an automatic verdict for Christianity as a whole.

At a historic church, ask whose name is on the memorial and whose labour, preaching and community building remain outside the inscription. A building photograph can establish a place; it cannot establish the beliefs of every person who used it. Recovering the congregation means following people and evidence beyond the architecture.

## The Jamaican diaspora did not begin at Tilbury.
Panama and the wider Caribbean basin restore a migration history that a Windrush-only narrative compresses. Work records reveal both movement and unequal treatment.

### The account under examination
1948 supplies the starting point for Jamaica’s modern history of people building lives overseas.

What the evidence changes: Windrush is a pivotal British episode within a much older and wider movement. Jamaican and other Caribbean migrants crossed regional borders, worked, formed families and encountered racial barriers before that arrival.

### A ship can become too large a symbol
The Empire Windrush’s 1948 arrival gives Britain a powerful commemorative scene. It does not supply the beginning of every Caribbean migration history. Our Windrush article retains that particular voyage, passenger evidence and newsreel interviews; this essay changes the geographical frame around it rather than diminishing the event.

Lara Putnam’s Radical Moves examines African-descended working-class migration from the British Caribbean into Panama, Cuba, Venezuela and North America after emancipation, alongside the barriers that developed in the interwar decades. Her account concerns a regional population; we must not relabel every migrant in it as Jamaican. [1]

### The canal was also a place people lived
US National Archives guidance identifies Jamaica among the islands from which West Indian workers came to the Canal Zone. It points to labour contracts, sailing lists and employment records, and also to marriage, birth and death records. The archive therefore offers a route into families as well as construction labour. [2]

An engineering achievement invites a story of excavation, locks and ships. A household history asks different questions: who travelled together, who followed later and where children were recorded. These need named records. We should not invent a representative Jamaican family and disguise it as history merely because the general circumstances are well documented.

### Opportunity came with unequal terms
The archivist’s account describes racial discrimination in jobs, wages, housing and working conditions. It also explains that surviving West Indian employment records are less extensive than the white employee records. Archival unevenness is part of the research problem, not a measure of which workers mattered more. [2]

This changes how absence should be interpreted. Failure to locate one person in an accessible series cannot establish that the person never worked there. Before drawing that conclusion, check the series’ coverage, spelling variants, jurisdiction and the possibility of commuting from outside the Canal Zone. A database’s empty result is a finding about that search, not a verdict on a life.

### A photograph should not assign nationality
Wellcome’s image of workers’ quarters at Cristobal, around 1910, places children and wooden dwellings in a landscape of segregated accommodation. Its catalogue identifies West Indian and Panamanian workers; it does not establish that the pictured children or every resident were Jamaican. The scene can support a discussion of housing while leaving those identities open. [3]

The distinction is more than a caption technicality. A picture circulated as “Jamaicans building Panama” can quietly turn a multi-island and Panamanian history into an exclusive national claim. Jamaica belongs in that history through the evidence; it does not need ownership of every face in the photograph.

### Movement could carry political ideas
Putnam connects migrant networks and popular culture with Black internationalism and anticolonial politics. That interpretation asks us to follow communication and affiliation alongside employment. A migration account made only of departure dates and destinations misses what people did with the relationships movement made possible. [1]

The revised starting point is therefore a network, not a single ship. Panama is one entry into it, and Windrush is another. Recover a person’s route through the records rather than assuming it followed a later commemorative timeline. The diaspora becomes both older and less geographically predictable when we stop making Britain its only destination.

## The postcard Jamaica was made, not simply found.
Krista Thompson’s work shows how photographs helped construct the tropical destination. Historical pictures must be read as authored evidence rather than transparent windows.

### The account under examination
Old tourism photographs simply show an untouched Jamaica before modern development altered it.

What the evidence changes: Photographers, promoters and colonial institutions selected and circulated a particular landscape. The picture can document a scene and a selling strategy at the same time.

### Beauty has a production history
Krista Thompson’s An Eye for the Tropics traces the marketing of Jamaica and the Bahamas as tropical paradises to the late nineteenth century. The publisher’s account describes promoters commissioning photographs and art for international circulation. What seems timeless had photographers, commercial purposes and a historical moment. [1]

That does not mean the sea was imaginary or the hills lacked beauty. It means a chosen view became a claim about the whole country. A narrow scene can be physically accurate while offering an inadequate explanation of the society around it. The error comes when we confuse what fits inside the frame with everything that existed.

### Look at what the frame asks us to admire
The Runaway Bay fishing photograph reproduced here is a particular image attributed to Dr James Johnston. Its catalogue suggests approximately 1890 while the available dating also places it before December 1921. Keep that uncertainty visible. It is not evidence of what every north-coast household looked like in one exact year.

Look at shoreline work as well as scenery. Then ask what the image cannot establish: earnings, land rights, the subjects’ consent, or their opinions of the photographer. Those questions are our reading exercise, not conclusions that can be extracted from the photograph alone. A visually compelling scene should prompt investigation rather than relieve us of it.

### The image could reshape the destination
Thompson’s publisher describes hoteliers importing plants to bring landscapes closer to the promoted tropical image and examines exclusion from tourist spaces. Her work thus asks us to study effects on everyday life as well as representation abroad. The destination and the picture could influence each other. [1]

The implication is not that every palm tree was imported or every hotel reproduced one policy. Those would require specific evidence. The useful historical question is how an expectation acquired practical force: what visitors were invited to expect, what operators supplied and which residents could use the resulting space.

### A tourist industry could also sell people
Elizabeth S. Manley’s 2022 research article, Runway Hospitality, explicitly examines Air Jamaica flight attendants and other women in the promotion of a harmonious national identity to overseas travellers. Its abstract supplies another period and medium for investigating the making of a destination image. [2]

A landscape postcard and an airline campaign should not be treated as identical evidence. They involve different institutions, technologies and audiences. Read together, however, they suggest a productive comparison: when national hospitality is represented through particular bodies and performances, what kinds of Jamaican life become easy to see and which become difficult to recognize?

### Our guide has to answer the same question
Tony’s Guide uses attractive pictures too. Scholarship does not exempt us from selection. If we feature only a beach, a great house and a smiling performer, adding footnotes will not repair the exclusions created by that sequence. Our editorial response is to place work, households, ordinary towns and political history within the journey, with the actual provenance of each image retained.

The reconsideration should change how you browse this site and how we build it. Ask who made a picture, when, where and for whom. Treat historical captions as claims to check. Enjoy a beautiful view while refusing to let it become the complete account of the people living beyond its edges.
