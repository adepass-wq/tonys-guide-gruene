HISTORY RECONSIDERED · 14 ESSAYS

Jamaica, reconsidered.

Fourteen substantial essays on what a familiar story gets wrong, leaves out or explains too simply. Read the actual treaty, follow the compensation, examine the market and listen to the people behind the cultural symbol.

Each piece identifies its claim and evidentiary status. These are questions tested against records and scholarship, not a survey claiming that every Jamaican believes the same thing.

1 OF 14 · Correction

Emancipation paid the enslavers. Freedom still had conditions.

The compensation ledger and James Williams’s account tell a harder story than a date on a national timeline: legal abolition, unpaid labour and the distribution of money were different events.

Read the complete piece →

2 OF 14 · Complication

The Maroon treaty secured freedom—and obligations to slavery.

A hard-won settlement protected a community. Its clauses also required the return of future fugitives and help against rebellion. Read those provisions together before turning Maroon history into either a spotless victory or a simple betrayal.

Read the complete piece →

3 OF 14 · Missing history

The economy enslaved Jamaicans built beyond the cane fields.

Provision grounds, markets and locally made pottery reveal knowledge, production and trade that a plantation-only picture leaves out. They also expose the uncomfortable fact that enslavers could benefit from the work people did to keep themselves alive.

Read the complete piece →

4 OF 14 · Scholarly reinterpretation

Morant Bay: an inquiry could condemn brutality and narrow justice.

Paul Bogle’s rebellion challenged land, taxation, representation and colonial power. Jonathan Connolly’s research shows how the official inquiry and legal controversy redirected that larger argument into questions about the governor’s authority.

Read the complete piece →

5 OF 14 · Missing history

The labour contracts behind “Out of Many, One People.”

Indian and Chinese Jamaican history is deeper than a list of cultural contributions. Ship returns, immigration files and carefully staged photographs connect belonging to recruitment, plantation power, settlement and the choices families made afterward.

Read the complete piece →

6 OF 14 · Correction of omission

The state’s apology belongs beside the Rastafari postcard.

Coral Gardens exposes a gap between cultural recognition and lived citizenship. Survivor testimony, a scholarly documentary and the government’s apology give us more to confront than a picturesque image of locks, drums and reggae.

Read the complete piece →

7 OF 14 · Complication

Jamaican music was never sealed off from the world.

Mento’s marketing names, imported records, local ritual, sound-system competition and Chinese-Jamaican studio networks complicate a pure, single-line history. The surprise is how much Jamaican originality depended on transforming connections.

HISTORY RECONSIDERED · ESSAYS 8–14 OF 14

History Reconsidered: the collection continues.

Indigenous heritage, war, women, spiritual authority, migration and the tourist image: familiar accounts change when we widen the evidence.

Our standard is a specific claim, scholarship tied to that claim, relevant documentary or visual evidence, and an honest account of disagreement and uncertainty. Surprise earns its place through evidence.

Read the second run in sequence →

8 OF 14

The Taíno story does not end neatly with “extinction.”

Archaeology, living heritage and genetic research ask different questions. Treating them as one verdict obscures both colonial catastrophe and the limits of what we know about Jamaica.

Read the essay →

9 OF 14

Tacky’s revolt was a war with more than one front.

Vincent Brown’s interpretation moves beyond one leader and one parish to examine African politics, Jamaican terrain and imperial conflict together.

Read the essay →

10 OF 14

1938 was made by workers. Women belong in the account.

Party leaders, wage records, oral testimony and Sistren’s theatre offer different views of the upheaval. Recovering women requires more than adding a heroine to the familiar political story.

Read the essay →

11 OF 14

Obeah: the accusation has a history too.

Court categories and colonial ideas shaped what counted as Obeah. Diana Paton’s research asks us to investigate the label as well as the people placed beneath it.

Read the essay →

12 OF 14

Black Jamaicans helped make Christianity their own.

George Liele, Moses Baker and Black congregations complicate a story in which Europeans supplied religion and Jamaicans merely received it.

Read the essay →

13 OF 14

The Jamaican diaspora did not begin at Tilbury.

Panama and the wider Caribbean basin restore a migration history that a Windrush-only narrative compresses. Work records reveal both movement and unequal treatment.

Read the essay →

14 OF 14

The postcard Jamaica was made, not simply found.

Krista Thompson’s work shows how photographs helped construct the tropical destination. Historical pictures must be read as authored evidence rather than transparent windows.
