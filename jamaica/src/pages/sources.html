Editorial method

Let the community point the way. Let evidence decide.

1. Capture a lead

Log a video's URL, creator, upload date, town, timestamp, claim and any named witnesses. Seek permission for media reuse.

2. Investigate

Find independent scholarship, manuscripts, maps, newspapers, records and alternative accounts. Distinguish direct testimony from memory.

3. Publish responsibly

Write a compelling narrative. Identify verified findings, interpretations, contradictions and open questions. Credit source creators and invite corrections.

Current status: Editorial research prototype. No user-submitted episodes have been processed. No access to historical manuscripts or photograph reuse permissions is implied. Travel particulars are not verified.

The Jamaica Language Atlas · Islandwide edition

Fourteen parishes. Many voices. No invented accents.

Explore Jamaica parish by parish, compare research questions and listen to recordings when an original source permits playback. This first edition is an islandwide research interface, not a completed dialect survey. Parish boundaries do not imply neat speech boundaries, and every community contains variation.

A parish index, not a geographical boundary map. Regional claims are deliberately marked as research questions until actual recordings have been evaluated.

St. Mary

Collection priority · Recordings not yet cleared for playback

What to collect

At least three speakers per locality across age, self-described language background and social setting; record the same short elicitation prompts, plus unprompted narrative. This is a minimum pilot goal, not a representative scientific sample.

Consent, community attribution choices, sensitivity review, transcript approval and recording rights must be recorded before publishing.

Compare the contexts

What should we actually compare?

Sound

Vowels, consonants, rhythm, intonation and speaking rate—measured from recordings, never inferred from class or address.

Grammar & vocabulary

Pronouns, tense-aspect markers, negation and idioms. Record examples in context; a shared word is not proof of a parish accent.

Social register

How speakers shift among Jamaican Creole, Jamaican English and intermediate forms depending on audience and setting.

Tony’s Call: Stop calling Jamaican Creole “bad English.” It has grammar, history and artistry. Equally, stop pretending there are fourteen fixed parish accents. The important story is how location interacts with schooling, mobility, class, age, performance and individual choice.

Historical voices: 1951–52

Frederic G. Cassidy recorded islandwide Jamaican speech during 1951–52; a library bulletin says copies of his research tapes entered heritage collections. This offers a possible historical comparison against modern interviews—not a verified online playback collection.

Learning studio: hear, read, speak, compare

Future lessons will present a real speaker’s authorized clip, a verbatim transcript, a Jamaican Creole spelling where suitable, a contextual translation, a sound/grammar explanation and a short listening exercise. Different speakers may express the same meaning in different ways; the site will not declare one community’s version “correct.”
