KROMANTI · MOORE TOWN & MAROON HERITAGE

Kromanti: a language history you can hear.

Study Kromanti through the Jamaican Language Unit’s documentary, named speakers, Maroon community history and a guided listening sequence.

The documentary introduces Kromanti, related to the Akan language cluster of West Africa, and Uol Taim Patwa, an older English-lexicon Maroon language variety. Follow the speakers and the documentary’s explanation before treating these as one language.

Moore Town’s language heritage belongs to the community’s ceremonies, music, history and relationships. UNESCO’s film places speech alongside drumming, movement and the use of the abeng. The landscape photograph locates that living community.

  1. Watch for context. Start with the UNESCO film to connect the language with Moore Town.
  2. Listen to the speakers. Use the UWI documentary, pausing at its explanations and demonstrations.
  3. Make a listening note. Record a sound or phrase, the named speaker and the explanation given in the film.
  4. Follow the evidence. Read the Kromanti article and the source-hosted Isaac Bernard recording discussed by the Omohundro Institute.

READ THE LANGUAGE LANDSCAPE

Language, dialect and accent are different questions.

Jamaican Creole / Patwa

A language with grammar, literature and a contact history. Much vocabulary is related to English; shared words do not make its grammar identical to English. UWI teaches its structure and expanded public uses.

Read the complete account →

Jamaican English

English used within Jamaica has local norms. Irvine-Sobers’s research studies variation in careful professional speech: speaking English does not require eliminating every locally meaningful sound.

Dialect, accent and register

Dialect can concern grammar and vocabulary as well as pronunciation. Accent concerns sound. Register concerns choices associated with a situation or audience. A speaker can vary without moving to another parish.

Maroon ritual language

Kromanti belongs to specific Maroon religious and community histories. It should not be treated as an interchangeable name for all Jamaican Creole, or assigned to every resident of Portland.

What location can—and cannot—explain

The UWI language-competence study conducted in 2006 used a sample of 1,000 people divided by eastern/western location, urban/rural setting, age and gender. It reported differences in demonstrated language switching across some of those categories, alongside effects associated with the interview situation. Those are sample findings about competence and interaction, not fourteen parish accent boundaries.

A parish is a useful place to locate a named recording. It is a poor substitute for the speaker’s biography, audience, date and situation. The map below consequently separates archival field locations from what a recording can demonstrate.
