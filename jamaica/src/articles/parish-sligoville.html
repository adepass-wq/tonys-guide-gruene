Sligoville: buying land before apprenticeship ended

Location: Sligoville, St. Catherine, Jamaica

Explore area in Google Maps ↗

Map search for the named place or area. Arrange the actual entrance, guide meeting point and access with your host; this is not a confirmed tour departure.

A named purchaser brings St. Catherine’s first free-village story out of a list of missionary achievements and into the lives of people making decisions.

The date matters

JNHT records that Baptist minister James Mursell Phillippo bought land in the St. Catherine hills in 1835, anticipating the end of apprenticeship. The property was divided into lots for formerly enslaved people. Do not collapse 1834 and 1838 into one undifferentiated freedom date. The land purchase belongs inside the intervening period, when people were already making material plans for greater independence. [1]

Name the purchaser

The Trust identifies Henry Lunan, formerly an enslaved headman on the adjoining Hampstead Estate, as the first lot purchaser. His name should remain beside Phillippo’s. A village is not made only by the person who acquires the larger property; it is also made by those who choose, finance and use individual holdings. The source identifies Lunan’s earlier position but does not give a complete account of his income, household or motivations. [1]

A name joins village and government

Sligoville was named for Howe Peter Browne, the second Marquis of Sligo and then governor. JNHT describes it as Jamaica’s first free village. That commemorative name points toward colonial government, while the individual lots point toward residents’ attempts to build lives with more control over land. Hold both in the picture. A place name can recognise an official without making that official the sole author of freedom. [1]

Follow the land rather than a slogan

The practical historical questions are about boundaries, payment, cultivation and residence. How large was a family’s plot? What document recorded a transfer? Did a purchaser remain there, and what changed in later generations? This introductory institutional account cannot answer every question. A responsible family-history exercise would follow the evidence through property and church records instead of treating a village’s foundation as a complete biography.

Compare without making identical villages

Maidstone in Manchester supplies a different documented purchase history, with instalments and terrain shaping an irregular village. Sturge Town in St. Ann provides another comparison. Shared words such as “free village” should help us ask related questions, not flatten these communities into copies of one another. Parish-specific history begins where dates, terrain, people and institutions differ.

Make a two-person timeline

Place Phillippo’s 1835 land purchase and Lunan’s first lot purchase on the same line. Under each, write what the source actually establishes. Then leave space for the evidence you would need to follow a household. On an arranged local visit, ask residents or a knowledgeable guide how the foundational story is preserved today. Treat contemporary testimony as a dated account of memory, and ask consent before recording it.
