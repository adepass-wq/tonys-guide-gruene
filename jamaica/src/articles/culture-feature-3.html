Burru: hear the rhythm, meet the people behind it

A drum pattern becomes a fuller history when we follow its players, Christmas routes, movement and changing audiences—from community practice to Rastafari music and today’s teaching demonstrations.

Start with the sound

A low stroke gives weight to the pattern; brighter strokes break across it. Joakim Lartey’s lesson isolates that conversation on a djembe, making it possible to hear how a repeating phrase holds together while the player changes emphasis. The producer writes the teaching sequence as two bass strokes, two tones, two bass strokes and four tones. Tap a steady pulse while listening, then notice the phrase returning.

This lesson travels with a Jamaican rhythm tradition, but its setting is a modern workshop in the United States. The distinction helps us hear transmission: someone has learned a practice, selected a pattern and made it teachable for another audience. A Christmas procession, a classroom and a Rastafari gathering give drumming different social purposes.

A Jamaican geography: Clarendon and St. Catherine

Jamaica Information Service associates Burru’s fertility masquerade with Lionel Town and Hayes in Clarendon. Its description gives movement a place beside sound: bent knees, rotating hips and intermittent jumps. The label consequently names more than an isolated percussion exercise.

[2]

A community account from Spring Village in St. Catherine supplies another scale of detail. It remembers Christmas performances moving from Rockstone to Bottom Pipe and names Albert Bryan, known as Sall Cup or Moochu, among the tradition’s carriers. Bryan organised the performances, kept the drums and composed lyrics; the account also remembers Wilbert as a singer. Here the rhythm had custodians, a route and a calendar.

[3]

The same recollection describes topical songs and village gossip. Such music could comment on conduct while gathering people into a shared response. Imagine the difference between listening from a chair and hearing a familiar name or incident enter a chorus in your own neighbourhood. That is an interpretive invitation, rather than a claim about an unidentified song in the workshop film.

Listen to the players together before trying to assign each sound a fixed label. A musical texture comes from relationships among parts, not just the presence of a drum. The demonstration allows us to follow that coordination, while the community account explains why a performance also mattered socially.

From Buru musicians to Count Ossie

Chelsea Stephenson’s institutional history for the African Caribbean Institute of Jamaica/Jamaica Memory Bank describes Buru musicians moving among rural communities and Kingston after emancipation. It also records social stigma around the practice. Musical skill could circulate even when the people carrying it faced contempt.

In that account, Count Ossie integrated Buru drumming into Rastafari Nyabinghi. The link names a musician and a new religious setting rather than making Burru, Nyabinghi and reggae interchangeable. It points to encounters through which older musical knowledge acquired new purposes and audiences.

[5]

The archive is another part of the story

JIS’s account of the 2006 folk-music digitisation project identifies recordings made under Olive Lewin and Marjorie Whylie during the 1960s and 1980s. Burru appears among the forms documented. Those research tapes and these publicly available demonstrations are different records of musical knowledge: one preserves field research, while the other makes a pattern available for teaching.

[6]

Carry three things away: the audible relationship of repeated and varying parts; the named people and places that sustained a tradition; and the changing occasions in which the sound was used. Burru becomes richer when all three remain in the story.
