St. Ann’s Bay and Ocho Rios: Who Gets Remembered on the North Coast?

St. Ann's Bay is a useful starting point for understanding administrative power, religion and historical memory. Its colonial townscape includes civic institutions, a parish church and a fort: the Jamaica National Heritage Trust dates St. Ann's Bay Fort to about 1750. In any town with an eighteenth-century fort and nineteenth-century administrative buildings, a history that talks only about architectural elegance has left out the people whose labor sustained the colony. [1]

Ocho Rios presents a different question. As a visitor hub, its present-day image often foregrounds beaches, excursions and hospitality. We should place those attractions alongside its older military sites, agricultural districts and changing patterns of landownership. JNHT notes that the Ocho Rios Fort was rebuilt and retains two original guns. St. John's Anglican Church near Ocho Rios is another important site; the National Library's separately dated photographs can anchor a carefully authenticated comparison, but the 1872 Fyffe–Kidston marriage still requires the original register. [2]

Historical memory is about selection. Which names appear on plaques? Where are laborers, enslaved ancestors, domestic workers, farmers and market traders represented? What buildings survived only because later generations could afford to restore them? This is a way to connect the built environment to modern economic inequality without claiming that all present-day differences have one cause.

Tony’s Call: Tourism can make a place visible while making much of its history invisible. Our job is to expand the frame, not condemn people for enjoying it.

Remember the ordinary lives around the monument

The fishing-boat photograph offers a useful counterweight to an account built only around commemorated figures. It shows a working coastline in 2023, not the nineteenth-century town. The boats make livelihood visible without establishing the histories of their crews. A responsible caption leaves that distinction intact.

Read the public memorials alongside the court-book and free-village essays. Those accounts widen the question from who is honored to who lived under particular laws, who bought land and who sustained the parish’s households. The point is not to remove named leaders from the story; it is to restore the people around them. For a practical day, St. Ann’s Bay and Seville can connect town observation with archaeological interpretation, subject to Seville’s actual opening schedule.

From a fort to a house of correction

The Heritage Trust dates St. Ann’s Bay Fort to about 1750 and records that its stone came from Sevilla la Nueva. Coastal encroachment led to the fort being declared useless in 1795 and a replacement at Windsor Point. The older site then became a jail and house of correction. That sequence joins colonial settlement, coastal change and punishment in one place. [1]

The account lists solitary cells, a treadmill and separate spaces for debtors, sick inmates and people described in historical language as lunatics. These details turn an architectural ruin into a social history of confinement. A picturesque wall does not reveal those institutions unless the guide explains them. [1]
