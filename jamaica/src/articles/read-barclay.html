A. A. Barclay and the struggle to keep more of the banana’s value

Barclay’s campaign joined credit, land, representation and marketing. The cooperative that followed was not simply a farmers’ club: it challenged who controlled the route from a Jamaican field to an overseas buyer.

The problem was larger than cultivation

In the early twentieth century, a small grower could produce bananas without controlling the contract, transport or overseas sale that determined the return. St. Mary’s agricultural economy therefore had a political question inside its business: who would represent producers when the companies purchasing and moving fruit held commercial advantages? Reverend Alexander Aemelius Barclay of Lucky Hill made organization a response to that imbalance. [1] [2]

Barclay was a Presbyterian minister associated with the Goshen circuit, an educationist and a political figure. Veront Satchell’s parish history connects him with a cooperative bank in Gayle, citizens’ associations, a land-settlement movement and the Jamaica Producers Association. These activities belong together. Land without credit could limit cultivation; a harvest without bargaining power could leave its producer dependent; isolated complaints might be less effective than organized representation. [2]

From a proposal to an association

Jamaica Producers’ institutional history dates Barclay’s cooperative proposal to 1924. It describes an effort to give small banana growers access to more sustainable export contracts, followed by a wider plan for marketing other agricultural products through a parent organization. In 1925, with F. H. Robertson, legislators, parish leaders and growers involved, the Jamaica Producers Association took shape. [1]

The sequence matters. The proposal was not merely to grow a better banana. It sought an institution between the individual farm and the external buyer. Coordinated production and marketing could change the terms of negotiation, but required growers to participate, officers to administer the organization and commercial expertise beyond the farm. Barclay’s leadership helped articulate the problem; it did not make the farmers passive beneficiaries.

Barclay did not live to see the 1929 cooperative

Satchell records Barclay’s election to the legislature in 1925 and his death in November 1926. That places an important boundary around his personal role. The Jamaica Banana Producers Association formed in 1929, after his death. It developed the organizing direction with which he had been associated rather than being an institution he personally guided through all its later milestones. [2] [1]

The later cooperative’s leadership combined farming, law and shipping experience. Jamaica Producers names Sir Arthur Farquharson, Charles E. Johnston and Captain S. D. List, and describes support from the Pringle family through commitments of fruit. This was a coalition with differences in scale and resources, not an organization made up exclusively of equally situated smallholders. Its ambitions extended from producing fruit to shipping and marketing it. [1]

Moving the fruit changed the scale of the struggle

The company chronology describes Robertson’s investigation of the British market in 1931 and the subsequent creation of a Jamaican-owned steamship operation with refrigerated vessels. Refrigeration and shipping were central to turning perishable produce into an export business. Collective selling on the island could only go so far if access to transport and the overseas market remained outside the organization’s influence. [1]

The corporate account also describes continued competition and a 1936 process involving a public meeting, a petition, a commission and an agreement with United Fruit, followed by conversion to a joint-stock company. That is a different organizational form from the original cooperative. The account should therefore be read as a history of changing institutions, not an unbroken story in which the founding solution remained the same forever. [1]

Why it belongs in the landscape of St. Mary

The story connects inland farms and towns to coastal trade and overseas markets. Churches and associations were places where representation could be organized; roads and ports moved a crop; contracts decided more than a scenic description of a banana field could reveal. A visitor seeing Port Maria’s waterfront is looking at a region whose agricultural history included both production and contests over commercial control.

The 1865 drawing here predates Barclay’s campaign and is not a portrait of the cooperative. It makes agricultural carrying work visible in an earlier Jamaican representation. Its contrast with the later institutions is useful: labor produced and moved fruit long before growers could assemble an organization capable of pursuing its value further along the chain.

The evidence supports the proposal, the organizational sequence and Barclay’s wider local role. It does not establish a numerical gain in every small grower’s income. Corporate histories explain how an organization remembers itself; they are less conclusive about how benefits and losses were distributed among different members. The historical achievement is the effort to change the bargaining structure. The measure of its results is a separate question requiring grower and financial records.

Tony’s Call Remember Barclay for organizing around the whole economic problem—land, credit, representation and sale. Keep his 1926 death distinct from the institutions that developed afterward.
