The postcard Jamaica was made, not simply found.

Krista Thompson’s work shows how photographs helped construct the tropical destination. Historical pictures must be read as authored evidence rather than transparent windows.

The account under examination

Old tourism photographs simply show an untouched Jamaica before modern development altered it.

What the evidence changes: Photographers, promoters and colonial institutions selected and circulated a particular landscape. The picture can document a scene and a selling strategy at the same time.

Beauty has a production history

Krista Thompson’s An Eye for the Tropics traces the marketing of Jamaica and the Bahamas as tropical paradises to the late nineteenth century. The publisher’s account describes promoters commissioning photographs and art for international circulation. What seems timeless had photographers, commercial purposes and a historical moment. [1]

That does not mean the sea was imaginary or the hills lacked beauty. It means a chosen view became a claim about the whole country. A narrow scene can be physically accurate while offering an inadequate explanation of the society around it. The error comes when we confuse what fits inside the frame with everything that existed.

Look at what the frame asks us to admire

The Runaway Bay fishing photograph reproduced here is a particular image attributed to Dr James Johnston. Its catalogue suggests approximately 1890 while the available dating also places it before December 1921. Keep that uncertainty visible. It is not evidence of what every north-coast household looked like in one exact year.

Look at shoreline work as well as scenery. Then ask what the image cannot establish: earnings, land rights, the subjects’ consent, or their opinions of the photographer. Those questions are our reading exercise, not conclusions that can be extracted from the photograph alone. A visually compelling scene should prompt investigation rather than relieve us of it.

Put another working landscape beside the shore

Compare Johnston’s Mandeville market with his Runaway Bay fishing scene. Both contain work, but the crowded inland trading space makes different demands on a viewer from the shoreline. The catalogue dates are approximate and these are not matched photographs of one locality. Their value is the comparison they permit: what would your account of Jamaica emphasize if it began with a market rather than a beach? Neither picture establishes earnings, ownership or the subjects’ consent.

The market is not proof that its photographer intended an argument against tourism. It is evidence that his pictured Jamaica contained other kinds of landscape. Selection happens again when an editor chooses which of those pictures to display. This guide’s use of the two scenes is an explicit editorial comparison, rather than an attempt to read unrecorded motives from the photographer’s mind. [4]

The image could reshape the destination

Thompson’s publisher describes hoteliers importing plants to bring landscapes closer to the promoted tropical image and examines exclusion from tourist spaces. Her work thus asks us to study effects on everyday life as well as representation abroad. The destination and the picture could influence each other. [1]

The implication is not that every palm tree was imported or every hotel reproduced one policy. Those would require specific evidence. The useful historical question is how an expectation acquired practical force: what visitors were invited to expect, what operators supplied and which residents could use the resulting space.

A tourist industry could also sell people

Elizabeth S. Manley’s 2022 research article, Runway Hospitality, explicitly examines Air Jamaica flight attendants and other women in the promotion of a harmonious national identity to overseas travellers. Its abstract supplies another period and medium for investigating the making of a destination image. [2]

A landscape postcard and an airline campaign should not be treated as identical evidence. They involve different institutions, technologies and audiences. Read together, however, they suggest a productive comparison: when national hospitality is represented through particular bodies and performances, what kinds of Jamaican life become easy to see and which become difficult to recognize?

Our guide has to answer the same question

Tony’s Guide uses attractive pictures too. Scholarship does not exempt us from selection. If we feature only a beach, a great house and a smiling performer, adding footnotes will not repair the exclusions created by that sequence. Our editorial response is to place work, households, ordinary towns and political history within the journey, with the actual provenance of each image retained.

The reconsideration should change how you browse this site and how we build it. Ask who made a picture, when, where and for whom. Treat historical captions as claims to check. Enjoy a beautiful view while refusing to let it become the complete account of the people living beyond its edges.
