Kenilworth: the sugar works behind the great-house view

Hanover’s surviving industrial walls lead into crop accounts, changes of land use and the difficulty of finding workers in records organised around property.

Search for Maggotty as well as Kenilworth

JNHT’s documented site history explains that the estate appeared in earlier records as Maggotty, including Maggotty and Top River in the 1757 crop accounts. It does not establish why the property later acquired the Kenilworth name. This is a practical lesson for local and family history: a modern site name can conceal the name under which earlier records were organised. A search using only “Kenilworth” risks missing the earlier account. [1]

Production was not a straight upward line

The Trust reports sugar output of 132 hogsheads in 1757, 231 in 1810 and 89 for the year ending December 1819. The figures offer a dated comparison in the stated unit. They do not by themselves establish output per worker, profit or a cause of decline. Hogsheads are containers, not a single weight we can safely convert without additional information. Keep the units as recorded and resist a neat economic explanation built from three values alone. [1]

What the industrial walls reveal

The source describes a two-storey mill house, elliptical window openings and parallel structures interpreted as parts of the processing complex. Some identifications are explicitly tentative, including a possible space for a waterwheel. Preserve those qualifications. A surviving opening can be measured or photographed; deciding exactly what equipment occupied it needs archaeological and documentary evidence. The interest of a ruin is that it invites this comparison between fabric and records. [1]

The accounts name an owner, but work produced the crop

Property histories commonly foreground owners, managers and overseers. That arrangement does not make the labour that cultivated and processed cane any less central. Ask what other records would name the enslaved people, their work and their households. An owner’s account can document a commercial result while failing to explain the human conditions under which it was achieved. The absence should guide the next enquiry rather than permit a fictional reconstruction.

The land continued after sugar

JNHT follows later property records into pasture, pimento, wooded or uncultivated land, tenant coconut growing and eventually banana and coconut use. The lesson is not simply that a sugar factory became a ruin. A property could continue to support different crops and arrangements after the industrial works ceased to define it. That longer sequence belongs in Hanover’s history of livelihoods and land use. [1]

Use a site comparison, not a beauty contest

Read Kenilworth beside Black River’s warehouses and Frome’s twentieth-century factory history. The first concerns an earlier estate works, the second a port and the third centralised industry and wage labour conflict. Comparing them makes different parts of a production-and-trade system visible. It does not prove one unbroken local enterprise or an unchanged labour regime across the centuries.

Try a record-and-ruin exercise

On paper, make a table with date, recorded name, output or land use, and source. Leave a column for what the entry cannot establish. Then examine the photograph and mark a visible architectural feature alongside the Trust’s level of certainty about its function. On an arranged visit, ask which interpretations are based on excavation, archival documents or a guide’s hypothesis. That is how a dramatic ruin becomes a serious learning experience.

Tony’s Call Best for an arranged Hanover industrial-history visit with permission from the present site manager. Allow around 45–90 minutes, a planning estimate, plus transport. Skip if you need a guaranteed step-free attraction or expect machinery demonstrations from the photograph. Confirm charges, permitted route, parking, structural restrictions, uneven surfaces and toilet access; do not climb ruins or enter closed areas.
