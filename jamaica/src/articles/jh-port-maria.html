Port Maria: The Courthouse Was Never Just a Pretty Building

Port Maria’s civic centre is a place to connect architecture with the changing exercise of public power. The waterfront, parish church, park and former courthouse belong to a working town, not a sealed colonial scene. Their significance becomes clearer when we follow how the civic building was obtained, used and rebuilt.

Land, rent and an 1821 courthouse

The Heritage Trust explains that public offices initially occupied rented private houses. After Thomas Manning’s 1816 bequest of Trinity land for the parish poor, legislation authorized an exchange of 90 acres there for the 54-acre Nibbs Penn property in Port Maria. The municipal buildings were erected on the latter site; the courthouse dates to 1821. [1]

This is a specific example of public administration taking physical form through property transactions. It also gives the reader a harder question than “Is the building beautiful?” How were public resources used, and who benefited from the arrangements? The source records the exchange; evaluating its consequences would require the underlying legislation and parish accounts. The architecture is visible, while those fiscal choices need research.

A twentieth-century political trial

JNHT identifies Alexander Bustamante’s 1942 manslaughter trial as an important episode here. Norman Manley joined the defence, and Bustamante was acquitted. The case brought political rivals and future national heroes into the same courtroom. [1]

The episode prevents us from freezing the courthouse in the slavery era. The same institution also belonged to the years of labour politics and emerging national leadership. A short heritage description cannot substitute for a full case history, but it can locate an event and explain why court records deserve attention alongside the façade. Ordinary litigants should also be part of that history when records permit.

A restored civic building, not untouched survival

The courthouse and police station were gutted by fire in 1988. The building was rebuilt and reopened as the Port Maria Civic Centre in 2000, retaining the Georgian style. [1]

What a visitor sees therefore includes deliberate restoration. A modern photograph cannot be treated as proof that every original interior or structural element remains. Start outside, compare the building with dated images and consider its relationship to the church and Claude Stuart Park opposite. Then connect the civic centre to the harbour and agricultural histories elsewhere in this guide. The town’s history is strongest when institutions, trade and residents are read together, with present-day access checked separately.

Tony’s Call The building has a history of land, law, political conflict and restoration. Give each layer its place; a handsome façade should introduce the story, not replace it.
