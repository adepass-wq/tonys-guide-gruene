Fort Charlotte: Lucea’s harbour, guns and a landscape that changed purpose

Hanover’s coastal fort becomes a study of harbour defence, changing technology and the later reuse of a military site.

The harbour gives the fort its meaning

A fort looking toward Lucea’s harbour belongs to the history of access from the sea. Its purpose was to defend a coastal position, not simply to provide a high viewpoint. Read the site beside the Lucea town article: a port joined commerce and movement, while military works sought to control the approach. That relationship makes the fort part of the town’s history rather than an isolated collection of old guns.

Keep the early date broad

JNHT places construction in the eighteenth century, during George III’s reign, and explains the name through Queen Charlotte. Its wording also says “mid eighteenth century,” without supplying a securely reconciled opening year. This guide retains the broader period rather than inventing a precise date. Names can connect a site with monarchy, but they cannot establish the date of every surviving wall or the sequence of alterations. [1]

Capacity is different from occupancy

The Trust describes barracks capable of housing fifty men, twenty-three gun openings and twenty mounted guns. Those are stated capacities and equipment figures, not a daily return naming the soldiers actually present. A garrison’s experience requires muster rolls, correspondence and supply records. Such documents might reveal absences, duties and shortages that a neat architectural description leaves out. The surviving fort offers the setting for those questions without answering them alone. [1]

The 1807 changes are a second layer

The account says that concern about the harbour’s defence during the Napoleonic Wars led to three twenty-four-pounder guns on traversing carriages being installed in 1807. The later installation belongs to adaptation, not the fort’s first construction. A traversing arrangement allowed a gun to change direction; the site record identifies surviving circular bases associated with that movement. Keep an equipment change beside its stated strategic concern rather than assuming all guns were installed at once. [1]

Architecture can explain an action

The photograph’s masonry and gun position make the relationship between structure and weapon more intelligible. A gun needed support, a firing opening and room for operation. Describe the visible arrangement, then consult a plan before reconstructing an entire battery. The term “twenty-four-pounder” describes a class of weapon; it is not the total weight of the gun or the range at which it could accurately hit a target. Avoid turning a label into unsupported specifications.

Defence protected a colonial order

A British coastal fort in plantation-era Jamaica did not defend an abstract landscape alone. Harbour security served a colony organised around property, trade and slavery. That is the wider context in which to read the fort, although the short site account does not identify every commodity or ship it protected. Compare Fort Charlotte with Kenilworth’s sugar works. One concerns a military installation and the other production; together they invite a history of how commerce and coercion related in Hanover.

Later education changes the site’s meaning

JNHT describes part of the fort as forming part of Rusea’s High School. That later use should be treated as an institutional layer, not as proof that the entire fort is presently open to visitors. A place made for military purposes can acquire educational and civic meanings. Ask how the school and local custodians explain the surviving fabric, and which areas are appropriate for a heritage visit without disrupting ordinary activity. [1]

Make a harbour diagram with two dates

Sketch the shoreline, fort and direction of the sea using the source photograph and a map. Add separate labels for the eighteenth-century foundation and the documented 1807 changes. Mark which features you can identify and which require a measured plan. Then read Lucea’s town story and Kenilworth’s estate account. The result is a local study of defence, trade and work, with different kinds of evidence attached to each part.
