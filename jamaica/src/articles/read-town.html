Before the resort coast: land, estates and the towns on Jamaica’s maps

The north coast was a working and governed landscape long before its present tourist economy. Maps and property returns show how ports, plantations, churches and smaller settlements were connected.

A map makes some things easier to see

Herman Moll’s 1717 map identifies settlements, churches and productive sites across Jamaica. It is a colonial representation of the island: the features selected and labeled reflect the world the mapmaker meant to communicate. A name or symbol could make a property visible while saying little about the households whose work gave it value. The image is useful precisely because it can be read as an argument about place, not merely as a decorative old map.

The National Library’s cadastral collection contains a different scale of evidence. Estate plans can show boundaries, cultivated areas, works, provision grounds and dwellings, and were created for purposes including transfer, inheritance and disputes. The institution’s account also identifies plans on which smaller holdings appear after emancipation. A map can therefore record a change in access to land as well as the layout of an owner’s estate. [1]

Parish borders did not isolate the economy

The 1817 almanac’s returns identify Kidston at Boscobell in St. Mary and Arthur’s Seat in St. Ann, while listing many other named holdings. Properties, livestock and enslaved populations appear within a parish administrative structure, but individuals and commercial relationships could cross parish lines. The same name in two parishes is a concrete reminder that a boundary on a modern map does not separate every family or business history. [2]

These printed entries are not a full account of residence. They identify a proprietor’s association with a property and the reported counts. To use them as a town history, we must keep that administrative purpose clear. They describe the concentration and distribution of claims over land and people; they do not say that the named proprietor personally performed the work or that everyone on a property belonged to one biological family.

A port was part of a chain of production

At Port Maria, Fort Haldane was erected in 1759 above a shipping harbor. The Heritage Trust names surrounding sugar estates including Llanrumney, Trinity, Quebec, Heywood Hall and Frontier. Defense and commerce were related: the fort’s position belonged to an economic landscape in which estates required routes to market. The waterfront was not just a picturesque edge of the town. [3]

Annotto Bay was another important commercial outlet. Satchell’s parish history connects its development to surrounding estates and describes regulation of its harbor. A town associated with shipping depended on people working well beyond the waterfront: cultivators, carriers, traders, boat crews and households supplying the wider system. The visible port was one concentrated point in that network. [4]

The town changed as the economy changed

Later banana production and trade reorganized parts of St. Mary’s commercial life. Barclay’s producers’ movement belongs to that transition: the crop’s value depended on credit, collective representation, shipping and external contracts. Contemporary associations with resorts do not erase those earlier connections. They add another economy to a coastline already shaped by production and movement. [5]

The 1861 Oracabessa illustration is useful in this context. It presents a street and buildings rather than the celebrity shoreline familiar in much modern travel coverage. It is an artist’s dated representation, not an inventory of every activity in the town. Read beside an estate return and a fort description, it helps restore the settlement as a place where people lived and did business before its present marketing identity.

A surname does not tell the whole origin of a community

Names such as Higgin Town invite questions about ownership and the people commemorated. The name alone cannot establish the founding date, every transfer of land or the identities of the residents who sustained the place. This guide does not present an unverified naming story as fact. The broader history can nevertheless be told: parish administration, estate tenure, post-emancipation settlement and trade provided the conditions within which towns and communities developed.

This is more useful than sending a reader to a directory with the instruction to assemble a story. The documentary relationships are explained here. The citations identify the records supporting them, while the specific naming question remains limited to what is actually established. A town’s history is not exhausted by the most visible proprietor’s surname.

What to notice on the north coast

A church, road junction, waterfront or old house can be read as part of those relationships. The church links worship to records and authority; the road connects households to services and trade; the waterfront joins agricultural districts to shipping; the house reflects resources, labor and changes of use. Their meanings are not identical, but each belongs to a wider social landscape.

The result is a north-coast history that precedes and accompanies the resort view. Beaches and scenery remain part of the place. So do the people who cultivated, carried, worshipped, bought plots and organized around unequal terms. Reading the map becomes a way of keeping those lives within sight.

The map's categories are part of its argument

A map can appear comprehensive because every place sits within one frame. Yet its symbols and labels select particular things for notice. Estate names, harbors and navigational priorities can be clearer than households, informal routes and the work of residents. Read that selection as part of the source, not as the natural order of importance.

The coastal town and the inland community belong in a chain of relationships. Production needs movement, exchange and institutions; a harbor needs something to ship. The regional articles let you follow those links without assuming each parish was economically sealed by its boundary.

For comparison, put the old island map beside a dated town illustration or recent waterfront photograph. Ask what each medium makes visible. A street view can show a building's frontage that an island map cannot; the map can show a wider connection absent from the street. Together they make the geography richer, provided the dates and scales remain separate.

Tony’s Call Use a town name as a starting point. Explain the land, work and institutions around it before reducing the community to the person named on a sign.
