The Taíno story does not end neatly with “extinction.”

Archaeology, living heritage and genetic research ask different questions. Treating them as one verdict obscures both colonial catastrophe and the limits of what we know about Jamaica.

The account under examination

A people declared extinct have no further place in Jamaican history beyond a few borrowed words.

What the evidence changes: Catastrophic conquest, surviving cultural practices, genetic ancestry and present-day Indigenous identity are different historical subjects. Evidence for one cannot automatically establish—or erase—the others.

Start with a settlement, not a disappearance

White Marl in St. Catherine offers a better beginning than an extinction date. Siegel, Beier and Kaiman describe a major pre-colonial settlement whose meaning extends beyond excavated objects to residents, heritage managers and Indigenous descendants. Their study examines the relationships between archaeological interpretation and living claims to the place. It is a study of heritage and continuity, not a genetic census of Jamaica. [1]

An inhabited landscape changes the questions we ask. Who selected a settlement location? How did households obtain food? What skills made a carved object or a pot possible? An opening sentence about disappearance can make all this social history feel like a brief prelude to the supposedly real history that arrives by European ship. That is a choice of narrative, not a requirement imposed by the evidence.

A useful name can hide differences

The Smithsonian’s account of a Lucayan duho, or carved seat, warns that the label Taíno can obscure cultural diversity across the Greater Antilles. Its featured object comes from the Turks and Caicos/Bahamas region, not Jamaica. Shared terminology helps comparison, but does not turn every island into one interchangeable society. [2]

The same care belongs in our pictures. A magnificent object from another island can illuminate a regional tradition when its actual origin remains visible. Relabelling it as Jamaican would make the page more dramatic while making the history worse. Regional resemblance is evidence to investigate, not permission to transfer an object’s provenance.

What DNA answers—and what it does not

Schroeder and colleagues sequenced an ancient individual from Preacher’s Cave in the Bahamas and identified relationships between that genome and Indigenous ancestry in some present-day Caribbean populations. This challenges a blanket regional claim that every biological lineage vanished. The ancient person was not excavated in Jamaica, and the findings do not establish the ancestry of every Jamaican or a particular Jamaican family. [3]

Genetic relationship is not the same as an unbroken language, a continuously recognized political community or a documented family tree. Conversely, a community’s heritage cannot be dismissed merely because no genetic study was found for its members. Each proposition needs evidence suited to it. A commercial ancestry category alone cannot resolve a specific seventeenth-century Jamaican relationship.

Keep catastrophe visible

Kenneth Morgan’s chapter on Jamaican Taíno history foregrounds settlements, craft, cultivation and religious practice, and notes a renewed public interest in Indigenous history. Its description of the surviving evidence differs from the DNA study’s research problem. Archaeology and historical synthesis recover ways of living; neither should be presented as a complete record of every person after conquest. [4]

Questioning an absolute extinction narrative does not reduce the violence of colonization. Nor does an assertion of survival establish that social life continued without rupture. The responsible reconsideration holds loss, transformation and the limits of surviving records together. It refuses the false choice between total disappearance and an unchanged people waiting intact to be rediscovered.

Continuity has Jamaican places and living speakers

Atkinson’s JNHT essay follows Indigenous heritage into named Jamaican places. At Rock Spring in St. Mary, the petroglyph called “One Bubby Susan” became part of later folklore; at Aboukir in St. Ann, wooden carvings acquired new meanings when people encountered them. These examples locate cultural encounters in particular communities. They do not demonstrate that every later belief is unchanged Taíno religion. The history lies partly in reinterpretation: an older object could enter another population’s spiritual world. [5]

Her account also identifies cassava processing, canoes and mound cultivation among Indigenous contributions. Technological knowledge can circulate between populations without preserving a separate political community. This gives continuity a practical dimension—food preparation and ways of working the land—while keeping it distinct from a claim about a particular person’s ancestry. [5]

Present-day people deserve more than discussion in the abstract. Erica Neeganagwedgin’s 2015 article draws on oral histories and the voices of ten people from two rural communities in southern Jamaica. She writes from an Indigenous research perspective about reclaiming Taíno identity and rejecting the language of nonexistence. The accessible abstract establishes the study’s method and scope; it does not supply the interview transcripts or a representative survey of Jamaica. Its importance here is that people identifying as Taíno are speaking in the present, rather than appearing only as figures in a colonial account. [6]

Visit with a better question

At Seville, use the guide’s archaeological history to connect Indigenous occupation with subsequent colonial landscapes. At White Marl, begin with the published heritage study rather than assuming an excavated site is an empty space available for tourism. A scholarly discussion of a place does not establish current visitor access or permission to enter it.

The question worth carrying is specific: what form of continuity is being claimed, and what evidence establishes it? A crop, a remembered place, a named descendant community and a DNA result may all matter. They do not all prove the same thing. That distinction gives Indigenous history a serious place in Jamaica’s present without inventing certainty.

Test the reconsideration

Choose one factual claim and one interpretation in this essay. Follow their references, identify the kind of source and explain what further evidence could change your conclusion.

Tony’s Call Do not use “extinct” as a shortcut that decides archaeology, ancestry and living identity in one stroke. Name the particular claim, then examine its evidence.
