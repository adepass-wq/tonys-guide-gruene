Una Marson: a Jamaican voice shaping the microphone

Her 1940 interview lets us hear a Jamaican woman conducting a broadcast. Follow the person behind the questions, then compare her radio work with wartime film and the voices that reached Britain’s public record.

She was making the exchange happen

Listen to Marson as an interviewer rather than waiting only for the guest’s answer. The BBC identifies this exchange with Ken “Snakehips” Johnson as recorded on 10 October 1940. It preserves a working broadcaster, asking and guiding within the conventions of an on-air interview. The clip lasts 117 seconds in the BBC’s version metadata. [1]

Three kinds of authority meet at the microphone: the broadcaster’s institutional role, the guest’s expertise and the audience's expectations. Listen for how Marson invites an answer and carries the exchange onward. That is a concrete way to hear agency. She is not merely an unnamed colonial voice inserted into a British narrator’s account.

Journalism, literature and organizing preceded the broadcast

English Heritage’s account describes Marson’s Jamaican journalism and her founding of The Cosmopolitan in 1928. It follows her move to London in 1932, work with the League of Coloured Peoples and subsequent broadcasting. The route to radio therefore passed through writing, publishing and campaigning. The institution did not create a public voice from nothing; it gave an experienced communicator another medium. [2]

This matters to the history of language. A speaker can address a studio audience in a public register without abandoning Jamaican identity. Do not use the sound of a question to measure someone against an imagined single “real Jamaican accent.” Compare what the person is doing, whom they address and what the occasion asks of them.

Speaking to home could also serve a wartime message

The BFI’s Hello! West Indies (1943) presents a Ministry of Information production built around the format of Marson’s program. Its description makes the staging explicit. Personal messages and collective wartime purpose can occupy the same scene; being heartfelt would not make a filmed presentation an unmediated private conversation. [3]

Watch who appears before the microphone, who introduces them and who is imagined listening far away. Then compare the 1940 audio. An interview organizes the contributions through questions; the film organizes them within a larger production. The difference is historical information, not a reason to discard either recording.

Jamaican voices in Britain before the famous arrival

The nurse’s account recorded in 1941 gives us another contemporary Jamaican voice, identified by her work rather than by a prominent biography. Place that beside Marson, then the 1948 Windrush exchanges. The sequence brings broadcasting, personal experience and arrival into one history of being heard. It also stops the gangplank image from becoming the start of every Caribbean life in Britain.

A short listening exercise

Listen once for the interview’s sequence and again for Marson’s questions. Write down one moment you can locate in the clip. Compare it with the nurse’s account: how does the task of asking differ from the task of relating experience? Record your observation before giving it a broader interpretation.
