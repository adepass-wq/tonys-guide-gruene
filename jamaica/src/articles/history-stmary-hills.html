Into St. Mary’s hills: Woodside, roads, farming and memory

Leave the coastal highway and read St. Mary through Woodside, Highgate and the routes joining farming communities to Port Maria and Annotto Bay.

A hill community holds several periods

Woodside’s heritage is unusually layered. JNHT identifies a Taíno petroglyph site in the Rock Spring area, an estate history, a former great-house site later used for schooling and community emancipation observances. [1] The layers do not mean every local tradition is an archaeological finding: the Trust distinguishes recorded rock carvings, historical documents and community understandings. That distinction makes the history stronger.

Think about what a school in an estate landscape means. A place once organized around ownership and labor could become part of a community’s educational life. The change is not just architectural; it alters who has reason to enter the place and what they can expect to gain there.

The road connected hills to the port

The Pencar bridge photograph, dated 1890–1895 in the archive, shows a road approach and a river crossing. It is a specific St. Mary object, not a generic “old Jamaica” illustration. Study the bridge and road as infrastructure: moving a crop, reaching a market or visiting relatives requires crossings as much as destinations.

Small farming belonged to a wider economy

Port Maria’s surviving civic and church buildings mark its past as a busy port. [2] The hills behind the coast should therefore be read in relation to export and trade, not as an isolated rural world. The Barclay article explains cooperative land, credit and the banana economy; the Highgate account follows a town connected to roads and farming. Households had to navigate the distance between what they could grow and the markets, transport and finance that made selling possible.

Memory is work done by a community

At Woodside, JNHT describes Daddy Rock as part of the community’s social memory and emancipation observance, including family plaques drawn from slave returns. [1] Such commemorative work connects named ancestors to a place. A visitor should not flatten it into a mysterious cave story: the point is the community’s relationship with history, records and remembrance.

A route worth doing slowly

Choose one inland community and arrange the visit. Highgate and a locally guided Woodside visit can be a stronger day than trying to cross the entire parish. Confirm travel time with the driver, expect winding roads and keep daylight for the return. Ask permission before entering a yard, photographing family memorials or walking toward a cave. Respectful access produces better history than treating every path as public.

On the drive, notice churches, shops, schools, gardens and additions to houses. They tell you where people gather, trade and invest. A building’s appearance alone cannot establish its age or its owner’s wealth; use what you see to ask better questions.

Tony’s Call: Get off the coastal road with an arranged welcome. One thoughtful visit in the hills will teach you more than a string of unintroduced stops.

A road carries household decisions as well as crops

The Pencar bridge is a useful starting point because infrastructure connects stories that are usually told separately. A farmer needs a way to reach a buyer; a pupil needs a way to reach a school; a family needs a way to visit, worship and obtain services. The archive photograph establishes a particular crossing, while the wider interpretation asks what dependable movement could mean. It does not identify all the travelers or prove how often they used the route.

Woodside adds a different kind of connection. A school on an estate landscape and memorial work using family records change what a place is for. The site's meaning is not exhausted by the people who once owned it. Education and remembrance give descendants and residents reasons to enter, interpret and claim the landscape. That is a historical transformation worth understanding, not a decorative “community story” placed after the main account.

Choose your inland stop according to the question you want answered. Highgate helps explain commerce and services; an arranged Woodside visit helps explain community memory; Port Maria supplies a coastal comparison. Do not promise yourself all three in a rushed afternoon. The best day leaves time for an explanation and a conversation, with permission, rather than a photograph at every sign. Read the housing article before the drive so that verandas and additions provoke questions about construction and changing use rather than guesses about class.
