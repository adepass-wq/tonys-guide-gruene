The House Was Never Just a House

Seville’s great house is not the whole plantation. The village sites bring the people who sustained the estate back into view. Here a house was a shelter, a working place and a small part of a community living under coercion. Its importance is not that archaeology can recreate every conversation. It is that the ground preserves evidence of lives that an owner-centred tour can overlook.

An imposed plan, a lived landscape

Armstrong and Kelly’s research follows changes in Seville’s African Jamaican settlement from 1670 into the late nineteenth century. Their central argument is that residents reinterpreted an imposed estate order, changing boundaries and domestic space while remaining within the plantation’s constraints. The pattern was neither complete independence nor passive acceptance. [1]

That gives a visitor a better question than “How many cabins were there?” Ask how the settlement related to the owner’s residence, production areas and routes through the property. Then ask what changed when people lived and worked within that arrangement. A plan records an organized landscape; comparing plans and excavations helps reveal that landscape’s social history.

The home could continue beside the dead

At House 16, a burial was found in the yard rather than inside the dwelling. The research reports continued use of the surrounding house-yard after the burial. At House 15, investigators did not identify a corresponding burial. These neighbouring sites therefore caution against assuming that every household followed an identical arrangement. [2] [3]

The interpretation is important without naming an ancestor, assigning a family relationship or reconstructing a ceremony. The evidence shows a relationship between burial and domestic space. It does not supply the dead person’s name or every belief held by the residents. Respect for that distinction allows human significance to remain visible without turning a discovery into folklore.

What to look for on a visit

Read an excavated plan, a reconstruction and a modern commemorative film as different things. The plan records surviving traces. A reconstruction combines those traces with an interpretation of missing elements. A Jubilee film documents how people remember emancipation today. None is interchangeable with a photograph of eighteenth-century household life. Together they can make the site much more intelligible: material survival, historical interpretation and public memory each do a different job.

Tony’s Call Begin with the village as well as the great house. The people who sustained the plantation belong at the centre of its history.
