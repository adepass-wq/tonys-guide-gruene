Black Jamaicans helped make Christianity their own.

George Liele, Moses Baker and Black congregations complicate a story in which Europeans supplied religion and Jamaicans merely received it.

← Essays 8–14 of 14Read in sequence →

The account under examination

Jamaican Christianity can be explained entirely as a European religion imposed on passive African recipients.

What the evidence changes: Colonial church authority and Black religious leadership existed in the same unequal world. Liele’s and Baker’s ministries show agency and transatlantic connections that a simple one-way transfer leaves out.

Separate the establishment from the congregation

The guide’s church history examines Anglican institutions within colonial taxation and government. That history matters. It cannot stand in for every denomination, preacher or congregation. The property of an institution and the ideas of a worshipper are related evidence, but neither can automatically substitute for the other.

Noel Leo Erskine’s research on George Liele identifies him as a pastor and educator in Kingston and Spanish Town and traces Moses Baker’s Baptist work in western Jamaica. It also connects Liele’s influence to David George and communities beyond the island. Those networks make Black people organizers and transmitters of religion rather than merely its audience. [1]

A journey carried obligations as well as belief

The Boston University biography describes Liele’s passage to Jamaica through a loan and indentured service, his arrival in 1783 and his later self-supporting ministry. His movements followed the upheaval of the American Revolution. Religious history here is inseparable from mobility, work and the practical problem of reaching another colony. [2]

The story changes when we begin with the person undertaking that journey. A church did not simply arrive as an abstract doctrine. Someone had to pay passage, find work, obtain permission and form relationships. A biography can recover those actions without suggesting that freedom from personal enslavement removed the constraints of a slave society.

An African American preacher complicates an imperial origin

Liele’s work preceded the better-known overseas missionary journey of William Carey. The Boston University account uses that chronology to challenge a conventional Baptist missionary starting point. We do not need to make Liele the exclusive origin of all Jamaican Christianity to recognize the importance of this correction. [2]

Chronology matters, but so does the category being compared. The first ordained person, first congregation and first overseas missionary are different claims. A national or denominational celebration can become inaccurate when it swaps one of these achievements for another. Specific credit is stronger than a sweeping superlative that the sources cannot establish.

Transformation is not simple acceptance

The religious history considered here asks us to distinguish doctrine, institutional allegiance and the lived interpretations of congregations. Being recorded as Baptist does not settle how a person understood spiritual power, authority or freedom. Nor does a European connection make a Jamaican congregation incapable of making its own choices.

This is an interpretation of why leadership and institutions should be studied separately, not a claim to have recovered every congregation’s beliefs. For such recovery, letters, covenants, preaching accounts and testimony would need to be compared. Liele’s 1793 account, discussed below through its later printed re-publication, makes some of those practical concerns visible. [2]

Liele’s letter brings the congregation into focus

Writing from Kingston on 12 January 1793, Liele reported building costs, books for the school, baptisms and land purchased in Spanish Town for a burial ground and meeting house. These details survive in a later printed re-publication of the correspondence. Read them as his report to a distant supporter, not an audited account of membership or finances. They describe a religious community acquiring materials, teaching, building and arranging burial: concrete work undertaken within a slave colony. [3]

Another letter, dated 12 April 1793, identifies Thomas Nichols Swigle as a deacon, schoolmaster and Liele’s helper appointed with the church’s consent. The congregation had participants with responsibilities, rather than one preacher and an undifferentiated audience. The correspondence also reaches readers in Britain. Black initiative and overseas connections were compatible; neither makes the history a simple story of isolation or passive importation. [3]

Salter’s Hill supplies a western comparison. JNHT attributes the church’s 1825 construction to enslaved Africans. The surviving ruin pictured here is a later view of that named Baptist site, not Liele’s Kingston meeting house. Religious institutions can be studied through the work of the people who built them as well as through those who preached inside. [4]

Avoid replacing one absolute with another

Black leadership does not acquit every church of complicity with slavery. Colonial entanglement does not erase every Black religious project. These conclusions can coexist because they concern different actors and structures. The historical work becomes more demanding when we abandon an automatic verdict for Christianity as a whole.

At a historic church, ask whose name is on the memorial and whose labour, preaching and community building remain outside the inscription. A building photograph can establish a place; it cannot establish the beliefs of every person who used it. Recovering the congregation means following people and evidence beyond the architecture.

Tony’s Call Look for Black leadership as carefully as colonial ownership. Jamaicans were participants in religious history, not containers into which somebody poured a faith.
