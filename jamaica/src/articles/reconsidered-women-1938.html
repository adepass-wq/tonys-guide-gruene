1938 was made by workers. Women belong in the account.

Party leaders, wage records, oral testimony and Sistren’s theatre offer different views of the upheaval. Recovering women requires more than adding a heroine to the familiar political story.

The account under examination

The labour rebellion can be adequately explained through the biographies of its best-known male political leaders.

What the evidence changes: Leadership matters, but the movement’s social history also requires workers, households and women’s experiences. A later performance can challenge public memory without proving every incident it dramatizes.

Begin before the platform speech

Colin Palmer’s Freedom’s Children connects the labour rebellion and its aftermath to the emergence of Bustamante and Manley and to constitutional and party politics. The publisher’s account also begins with workers challenging colonial conditions. Moving immediately to the two leaders would remove the people whose grievances made political leadership consequential. [1]

A leader’s speech and a worker’s decision to join a protest are different historical actions. So are keeping a household fed, losing wages and deciding whether the risks of confrontation are bearable. These are questions for the records, not an invitation to invent an unnamed woman’s thoughts. A fuller account must seek the social conditions that a political biography can leave outside its frame.

Frome, before the story became a party history

In the Commons on 4 May 1938, the colonial secretary relayed the governor’s account of Frome: a wage dispute on 29 April, a strike by factory-construction workers the next day, and police firing on 2 May. The report recorded four people killed. Those are figures and descriptions in an official report, not an independently reconstructed final casualty total. Its characterization of the trouble as local also shows how government initially bounded the event. The later islandwide upheaval cannot be compressed into that early explanation. [4]

The same exchange contains MPs pressing questions about hunger, labour conditions and representation. It exposes disagreement inside the imperial record itself. A transcript can preserve an official justification and a challenge to it on the same page. Read the questions as well as the minister’s answer; neither side’s language should silently become the guide’s neutral narration.

The National Library’s catalogue explicitly identifies men and women in this scene of labour unrest. That is direct visual evidence of a mixed crowd; it cannot identify their occupations or establish who led an action. It nevertheless gives the essay something the two-leader portrait cannot: women visibly present in the public history we are discussing. [5]

Oral history changes who can enter

UWI’s Social History Project identifies Not for Wages Alone, edited by Patrick Bryan and Karl Watson, as a collection based on 35 oral testimonies about the conditions surrounding 1938. It also lists Linnette Vassell’s Voices of Women in Jamaica, 1898–1939. These are distinct research resources; their existence does not mean their full contents are openly available online. [2]

A remembered event should be assessed through the speaker’s position, the interval before the interview and comparison with other evidence. Those questions strengthen oral history rather than disqualifying it. Official dispatches also have authors, audiences and purposes. Treating a governor’s report as neutral while dismissing a worker’s recollection as merely subjective would impose an unequal evidentiary standard.

A woman at the centre of a later retelling

Karina Smith analyzes Sistren’s 1985 production Ida Revolt inna Jonkonnu Stylee. The repository abstract describes its woman-centred retelling and identifies the story that Ida Homes threw an initiating stone as a rumour. That qualification must survive our retelling. The play is evidence of how the past was reworked in 1985, not independent proof of the alleged stone-throwing. [3]

There is a valuable surprise here. Correcting an exclusion does not require certifying a dramatic origin story. We can examine why the production put a woman at its centre, what theatrical traditions it used and which questions it raised. That makes the performance historically meaningful without changing its evidentiary category to satisfy our desire for a tidy beginning.

Work is larger than the wage packet

The Frome article supplies a documented local sequence and an official report to inspect. Follow the dispute there before generalizing it into the entire island’s rebellion. A photograph of the completed factory belongs to industrial history; it should not be labelled as the confrontation during construction. A learning chronology should likewise remain labelled as an editorial graphic.

To investigate women’s participation seriously, compare named workers, occupations, household circumstances and the political language used about them. Ask which forms of labour the source counts and which it overlooks. A claim about unpaid household work should be grounded in evidence about actual households, not inserted as a universal explanation that bypasses local differences.

Restore the people without erasing leadership

The point is not to replace two famous men with one newly celebrated woman. It is to change the structure of the account so collective action, social conditions and subsequent memory can all be examined. Party organization and constitutional change remain consequential; they become more intelligible when we recover the population to whom politics had to answer.

Our conclusion is deliberately bounded. The sources reviewed establish a serious scholarly and documentary route into workers’ and women’s histories, and a later theatrical challenge to public memory. They do not supply a verified roster of every woman present in every protest. That roster’s absence from this review is a research limit, not proof that women were absent.

Tony’s Call Keep the leaders in the story, but make room for the people who made leadership necessary. Do not promote theatrical memory into a verified eyewitness event.
