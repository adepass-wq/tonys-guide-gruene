Inside the Household: Sleeping, Kinship and Privacy

A small house does not tell us how small a family was. Nor does a two-room plan tell us where its children slept. At Seville, archaeology lets us describe particular domestic spaces more securely than we can identify the people who occupied each room. That distinction matters: the inhabitants deserve a history, but they do not deserve invented intimate details.

A home with an outdoor life

House 16 had two rooms within a structure approximately five metres by three metres. Different flooring and surviving doorway evidence help establish that layout. Investigators also identified a probable porch facing the village path, and a yard with hearths and prepared marl surfaces. Their interpretation places much household activity beyond the walls. [1]

This changes how we read “crowding.” Counting the floor area alone may miss working and social space outdoors. Yet counting the yard as usable space must not turn imposed poverty into a romantic picture of freedom. A useful reading asks who could enter, where a household conducted its activities, and what protected those activities from observation. The dimensions answer the first architectural question; they cannot settle the social ones.

Rooms, relationships and the right to be unseen

Armstrong and Kelly interpret the reworking of Seville’s settlement as a way residents created some autonomy within an estate landscape organized by owners and managers. That is an argument about spatial power, not proof that every room was private or that families controlled where they lived. [2]

For family history, keep three questions separate: who was related; who lived at the same stated place; and who actually shared a dwelling. A marriage entry can name relationships and residences without establishing sleeping arrangements. An archaeological plan can locate rooms without naming their users. Agreement between different records strengthens an interpretation; absence from one record does not erase a relationship.

A careful household reconstruction

Start with the dated record, then place each person alongside the residence, occupation and witnesses actually recorded. Add a house plan only when there is evidence linking that household to that building. If an oral account describes a sleeping space, record who remembered it, when they lived there and which generation they were describing. Present a recollection as a recollection. The goal is to recover changing family lives, not to furnish an imaginary room.

Privacy is a relationship, not a room label

The two-room plan establishes divisions within House 16. It does not identify a bedroom, a children's room or the people present on any night. Calling a space private also requires more than a wall: we would need to know who could enter, what activities occurred and how the household related to estate oversight.

This is where family records and archaeology can complement each other. A register may name kinship and residence; a plan locates a dwelling and its features. Their information does not become interchangeable simply because both concern domestic life. A reliable reconstruction keeps the links explicit and leaves unproven sleeping arrangements open.

Compare the image of residents outside houses with the measured Seville plan. One makes people visible without showing their interior; the other makes structure visible without naming its occupants. Together they teach why an ordinary home deserves several kinds of evidence. That is richer than a vivid invented scene of who slept beside whom.

Tony’s Call The honest reconstruction is more valuable than the vivid one. Describe the house we can document and the family we can trace; leave sleeping arrangements open when the evidence does.
