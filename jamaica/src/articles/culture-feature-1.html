Kromanti and Maroon sound: Music, memory and the politics of Maroon freedom

Kromanti-language ritual practice and Maroon musical traditions belong to histories of African continuity, political autonomy, and negotiation with colonial power. The music cannot be detached from community authority, spiritual knowledge and the uneven historical experiences of different Maroon settlements. [2]

The 1739–40 treaties protected specific Maroon communities while imposing obligations to return certain runaways. Maroon music should therefore not be used as sentimental proof that the communities were always universal liberators. Neither should the treaty obligations erase their earlier military resistance or ongoing cultural distinctiveness. [3]

Compare Accompong with Moore Town

The two recordings embedded here deliberately identify different communities. Wah Me Gwine Do? is credited to Maroon musicians of Accompong; Shedo is credited to the Moore Town group. Start by hearing differences in vocal entry, repetition and ensemble texture. Do not infer a whole community’s ritual practice from one excerpt, or rename both performances simply “African drumming.” Their catalogue identities matter because Jamaican Maroon history is plural.

UNESCO’s public account connects Moore Town heritage with local authority, communal land, the abeng and Kromanti ceremony. Music sits within those relationships rather than outside them as an entertainment product. The abeng is particularly instructive: a sound can communicate across distance as well as appear in a recording. To understand a signaling practice we would need knowledge of who can play it, what particular calls mean and the occasions on which they are used. An isolated horn sample cannot establish all of that.

Read the treaty essay before deciding that any recorded performance proves a simple political message. The legal documents and the living heritage illuminate different parts of the story. Contemporary bearers are not interchangeable with eighteenth-century signatories, and cultural survival does not erase the coercive obligations of a historical agreement. A more rewarding visit listens to community interpretation and treats permission, local leadership and historical specificity as part of the experience. The public recordings offer an introduction; they are not a substitute for those relationships.

Recognition does not make every Maroon practice interchangeable

Accompong and Moore Town have particular community histories. A public recording from one cannot stand as an accent sample, ritual description or musical inventory for the other. Keep the performer, place and occasion beside the sound before making a comparison.

Kromanti's relationship with memory and ritual requires the same care. A language-learning resource can introduce a subject without giving a visitor unrestricted access to ceremonial knowledge. The living bearers decide what may be taught, filmed and shared. That authority belongs in the historical account, not only in a visitor etiquette note.

Read the treaty next to the performance. One documents a negotiated political settlement; the other documents sound at a later occasion. The connection is community history across time, not proof that every phrase or performance reproduces the eighteenth century. The language atlas offers another route into the distinction between location, everyday speech and a named heritage language.

Tony’s Call: Listen to community explanations of ritual context. Compare surviving treaty documents to later accounts of Maroon heritage. Do not assume every recording or festival rendition belongs to a single unchanging tradition.
