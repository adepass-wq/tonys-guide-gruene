JAMAICA, RECONSIDERED · 2 OF 14 · COMPLICATION

The Maroon treaty secured freedom—and obligations to slavery.

A hard-won settlement protected a community. Its clauses also required the return of future fugitives and help against rebellion. Read those provisions together before turning Maroon history into either a spotless victory or a simple betrayal.

The claim under examination

“The treaty was an uncomplicated victory for the freedom of all enslaved Jamaicans.”

What the evidence changes: Cudjoe’s agreement protected named communities within a colonial order that continued to enslave others. Freedom, land, commercial permission and coercive obligations coexist in the document.

The victory was real. Read whom it covered.

The second article promises liberty to Cudjoe, his captains and people, while addressing people who had recently joined them in a more qualified way. The third specifies land. Those were consequential gains wrested from a colonial power after sustained conflict. But a promise to particular communities is not a promise to every enslaved person in Jamaica. The distinction lies in the wording of the settlement, not in a modern effort to diminish resistance. [1]

The agreement reproduced by the Assembly carries the date 1 March 1738–9. The paired year reflects historical dating practice; it should not be silently converted into a different festival date. The text reproduced here is Cudjoe’s western settlement. It cannot stand in for every eastern agreement or every Maroon community’s history. Local specificity is the first protection against an islandwide myth built from a single document.

The clause that an uncomplicated victory cannot absorb

Article nine · read the obligation

“That if any negroes shall hereafter run away from their masters or owners, and fall into Captain Cudjoe’s hands, they shall immediately be sent back to the Chief Magistrate of the next parish where they are taken; and those that bring them are to be satisfied for their trouble, as the legislature shall appoint.”

Historical classification retained from the 1739 agreement. This clause concerns future runaways; article two and article ten address other categories. Examine the full primary transcription ↗

The confirming statute gives the arrangement an administrative mechanism: the magistrate was to pay ten shillings for a fugitive delivered by Cudjoe or people under his command and arrange onward custody. The exact payment is in section II of the act, not the literal wording of article nine. That distinction matters because a paraphrase can make a correct general point while misrepresenting where the evidence sits. [1]

Article six adds an obligation to assist in suppressing rebels, with a qualification concerning acceptance of the settlement’s terms. Article seven concerns defence against foreign invasion. Read together, the provisions connect Maroon security to services valuable to the colony. The colonial government did not simply accept an independent refuge and disappear. It sought an organised relationship with communities it had been unable to eliminate.

Land and commerce were regulated, too

Article four permits agriculture, stock raising and sale of produce while requiring a magistrate’s licence when commodities are brought to market. Later clauses concern roads, annual appearances when required, resident white intermediaries and succession. The text therefore tells a story of negotiated room to live and limits on that room. Counting only the acres misses the network of obligations surrounding the land. [1]

The guide’s interpretation is that this was a settlement of power, not a modern declaration of universal human rights. The people negotiating wanted survival, security and recognized space for their descendants. The colony wanted peace and assistance in maintaining order. Those interests overlapped in places and conflicted in others. That structure helps explain the document without making each party’s motive identical.

Scholarship goes beyond the two familiar verdicts

McKee’s article shifts attention from an exclusive story of resistance or betrayal to interactions and alliances between Maroons and nearby white settlers. Its stated scope is 1739–1795. Siva’s thesis abstract likewise examines post-treaty social, economic and demographic history and highlights policing obligations. These research questions are useful because a treaty starts a relationship; it does not end history. Implementation, trade, disputes and internal change remain to be studied. [2]

The distinction between obligation and practice is essential. A clause establishes what the agreement required. It does not, alone, tell us how frequently it was enforced, whether every captain behaved alike, or how every enslaved person encountered Maroons. Siva’s wider historical project points toward those questions. We should not calculate a record of all fugitive returns from one paragraph of legal text. [3]

A living community is not a colonial contract

UNESCO’s account of the Blue and John Crow Mountains emphasizes refuge, trails, spiritual connections and living Maroon heritage, including Moore Town. That complements the legal text without making the western agreement a description of all eastern practices. Contemporary community knowledge, ritual and land relations require their own identified evidence. A person living now should not be reduced to the most troubling provision in an eighteenth-century agreement. [4]

The named Moore Town and Accompong performances on Drums of Defiance supply another kind of record. They document artists and occasions, not the negotiations themselves. Hearing cultural continuity is valuable; using a twentieth-century performance to prove what was sung in 1739 would be an unsupported leap. The article links the guide’s listening resources so the legal and cultural histories can be studied together. [5]

Tony’s Call Respect the achievement enough to read its terms. A freedom story that omits article nine is incomplete; a betrayal story that erases the struggle and the living communities is incomplete as well.
