Before 1950: Jamaicans speaking, broadcasting and being heard

Hear a Jamaican interviewer in 1940 and a nurse’s account in 1941, watch the short exchanges at the Windrush arrival in 1948, and compare them with older field recordings and wartime broadcasting. The occasion changes what a voice can tell us.

Start with the person who asks the questions

Una Marson is often introduced through a list of achievements. A recording lets us encounter her doing the work. The BBC preserves her interview with bandleader Ken “Snakehips” Johnson, dated 10 October 1940; its clip metadata gives a duration of 117 seconds. Marson is the Jamaican voice to follow in this exchange. The recording is a broadcast interview, not a private conversation caught accidentally by a microphone. Its value is that we can study an actual exchange rather than hear an actor perform an imagined historical accent. [1]

First listen for who starts a turn, who answers and where the rhythm of the exchange changes. Then listen to Marson’s questions. Addressing a guest before an audience is a different task from chatting with family. We should not rank one as a more legitimate Jamaican voice. A public register is part of a speaker’s repertoire. The BBC’s description identifies the interview; it does not establish that every question and answer was unrehearsed.

English Heritage identifies Marson as Jamaican-born and describes her movement between journalism, literary work, campaigning and broadcasting. That history puts the voice in an institutional setting: she was helping decide who could speak on air, not simply supplying a picturesque voice for someone else’s account. [2]

1941: a working woman describes the Blitz

A second audio selection brings us closer to an ordinary person’s experience. CD41’s original release notes identify A Nurse in the Blitz as an unnamed Jamaican nurse describing the bombing of London, recorded on 1 January 1941. The publisher lists 4 minutes 23 seconds; the streaming edition lists approximately 4 minutes 24 seconds. This is a contemporary account rather than a later interview remembering the war. The available description does not establish whether the account was scripted or entirely spontaneous. [8]

Her unnamed status should not make her marginal to the lesson. Follow the sequence of events she describes and how she places herself within it. Work and danger can become visible through a speaker’s own account, even when a catalog supplies less biographical detail than it does for a prominent broadcaster. A title is not enough to give her a birthplace, parish, age or full life story; those remain open questions.

Compare the nurse with Marson by asking about task, not status. One person recalls experience; another conducts an interview. Both are recorded public voices, with different relationships to an audience. Listen for specific moments of emphasis or narration, then return to the historical setting instead of calling one voice more Jamaican than the other.

1948: a reply to a reporter, framed for a cinema audience

British Pathé’s Pathe Reporter Meets (1948) contains the Windrush arrival sequence after an unrelated opening celebrity interview. Its short passenger exchanges offer a different setting from Marson’s studio: a reporter, newly arrived travelers and a camera producing a public story. The newsreel was released on 24 June 1948. Listen to the replies separately from the commentator’s explanations of them. [3]

These are filmed question-and-answer exchanges, edited into a newsreel. They are useful evidence of speech in that encounter, while the cut does not reveal the whole conversation. The film’s descriptions of passengers as Jamaicans are not an item-level birthplace record for every unnamed person. Keep names and origins open where they are not established. Lord Kitchener’s contribution is singing by a Trinidadian performer; it must not be used as a sample of a Jamaican person speaking naturally. [4]

For learning, follow one answer before trying to compare accents. What question was put to the speaker? What did the speaker choose to explain? What does the reporter or commentator add? Those are three contributions to the source. The recording is more informative when the reply is allowed to remain a reply, rather than treated as a voice representing an entire island.

1921: early voices, with a different kind of evidence

Indiana University’s Roberts and Beckwith collection preserves Jamaican wax-cylinder recordings from 1921. The public examples include Mrs Swabie singing a Jonkonnu song at Prospect, Watson’s Hill, and an unidentified fife-and-drum performance at Brown’s Town. The catalog describes English and Creole in sung/spoken material, but the public examples identified here are performances. We have not established a publicly playable ordinary conversation in this collection. [5]

Use the song to compare delivery and occasion, with the singer and locality attached. It is a valuable early human voice; its words are organized as a song. The full catalog includes story-song and ritual categories, and its full online access is restricted to authorized users. An archive’s early date does not turn all of its contents into everyday speech. Follow the public sample without substituting a later reenactment for an inaccessible original.

1943: personal address inside a managed wartime film

The BFI presents Hello! West Indies (1943) as a Ministry of Information film using the format of Marson’s radio program, including messages to people in the Caribbean. Its description calls the messages stage-managed. This is a useful comparison with an interview: speakers can address home in a personal way while the production organizes the occasion for a public purpose. [6]

Watch for the relationship between speaker, microphone and intended audience. Do not label every Caribbean participant Jamaican. The related IWM record distinguishes West Indies Calling, the shorter version for British audiences, from the longer film; the two titles and cuts should remain distinct. [7]

Make the comparison useful

Move from Marson’s interview to the passenger exchanges, then choose a 1952 occupational conversation already in the language atlas. The later recording is outside this article’s pre-1950 selection and is labeled as a comparison. Hold task and setting in view: a broadcaster asks a guest questions, a traveler answers a reporter, and a worker explains an activity to a field researcher. Differences may concern audience, recording conditions or individual choice as well as language.

Tony’s Call: Start with a real exchange, learn who is speaking and what they are doing, then compare. Don’t ask two minutes of broadcasting to stand for every Jamaican household in the 1940s.
