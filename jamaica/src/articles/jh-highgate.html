Highgate, Richmond and the Inland Towns: Where Rural Jamaica Did Business

St. Mary is often explained from the shoreline inward. That approach misses the towns whose markets, schools and roads tied upland cultivation to coastal shipping. Official parish accounts describe Highgate as a busy agricultural and commercial center and Richmond as an older farming and residential settlement, formerly known as Meeks Springs. Those descriptions are starting points for deeper town histories rather than proof that all inland communities developed identically. [1]

Tony’s Call: Rural market towns are not minor footnotes to Jamaica's tourism story. They are where small farmers confronted the daily economics of independence.

Evidence: Official St. Mary town overview; St. Mary economic history. Individual market dates and historic photographs require further verification.

Put the inland towns back into the route

A travel account centered only on beaches risks turning the interior into blank space between attractions. Highgate and Richmond invite a different reading: places where rural households connected to services, transport, trade and institutions. The task is to identify those relationships locally rather than use “market town” as a decorative label.

A historic island map is helpful for comparison, but the 1717 map shown here must not be treated as a street plan of the present towns. It records the mapmaker’s island-scale priorities, including settlements and productive sites. Modern businesses, road alignments and public facilities belong to a later chronology. Read the differences before deciding that an old symbol denotes the same place or function today.

The agricultural-cooperation article offers a strong connection: production in surrounding districts had to reach buyers through physical and institutional routes. That gives an inland stop a purpose beyond taking a photograph. Ask how people reach a school, a bank, a market or a place of worship. The answers locate everyday life within the region’s history, while current opening and transport arrangements still need local confirmation.

A crossing in the archive: Pencar River Bridge

This late-nineteenth-century photograph gives inland movement an object you can examine. The road approaches a bridge through palms, and the printed album caption identifies Pencar River Bridge in St. Mary. A bridge turns a river from a recurring obstacle into a crossing point around which journeys can be planned. That is a useful way into the history of markets, schools and settlements beyond the coast. [Archive]

Look at the dirt approach as well as the bridge itself. Building a span did not make every route a paved modern road. People and goods still had to reach it from farms and villages. The photograph does not show enough traffic to identify a banana shipment or a particular trader; its evidence is the crossing and its setting. It helps explain why a parish’s economic history must include routes between places as well as the ports at the end of them.

Credit and settlement connected the inland towns

Satchell dates the Western St. Mary Co-operative Loan Bank in Gayle to 1914 and the St. Mary Benefit Building Society in Annotto Bay to 1916. He identifies Barclay and Edward James Touzalin respectively with their foundation. These were different institutions, but both show rural economic life being organized through finance as well as cultivation. [1]

His account also connects the 1990s passenger-railway closure with lost livelihoods in Annotto Bay and Richmond, and to a lesser extent Highgate. Inland towns did not simply remain unchanged while the coast developed tourism. Changes in transport altered the work and connections on which residents relied. [1]

Highgate and Gayle appear in this history as commercial, educational and religious centers. Housing on former estate lands added another population to the surrounding districts. The combination explains why a town’s history cannot be reduced to a founding date: its market, schools, churches, transport and residential growth connect different generations to different economic arrangements.
