JAMAICA, RECONSIDERED · 3 OF 7 · MISSING HISTORY

The economy enslaved Jamaicans built beyond the cane fields.

Provision grounds, markets and locally made pottery reveal knowledge, production and trade that a plantation-only picture leaves out. They also expose the uncomfortable fact that enslavers could benefit from the work people did to keep themselves alive.

The claim under examination

“Enslaved Jamaicans appear in economic history only as labour supplied to plantations.”

What the evidence changes: They also grew food, made goods and participated in exchange networks. The room they created for themselves remained entangled with slavery’s demands and costs.

The plantation account has a blind spot

If the history contains only cane, sugar, estate owners and export tonnage, enslaved people enter it as a labour input. That account describes an important coercive industry. It leaves out the practical work of producing meals, tools, vessels and relationships. The omission matters because survival was not provided automatically by the system that demanded labour. A household’s struggle to reproduce itself belongs inside economic history, rather than in a decorative paragraph about customs.

Edwards, Plummer and McKenzie examine provision grounds across a long Jamaican history. Their article stresses a contradiction: cultivation could sustain foodways and create room for independent action while also reducing the proprietor’s feeding costs. They distinguish survival and resistance from an imagined space wholly outside plantation power. The useful surprise is not that a slave system secretly allowed freedom. It is that people exercised knowledge and initiative inside conditions designed to exploit them. [1]

A provision ground is not a gift of leisure

The term may sound modest, but it names a relationship among land, time, food and power. Where a household could cultivate, how far it travelled, and when it could work shaped what growing food required. The garden cannot be understood by counting crops alone. The labour demanded on the estate and the labour needed to keep a family alive occupied the same person’s finite day. A plot could support autonomy and impose another burden at once.

In this reading, selling a surplus can change a household’s options without ending its subjection. Money from a crop might support a purchase, a relationship or a plan. It did not dissolve the legal claims and punishments surrounding the seller. We should therefore avoid two errors: erasing initiative because slavery was coercive, and making initiative an excuse to soften the coercion. Both elements belong in the same account.

Pots let us investigate connections that a ledger misses

Hauser, Descantes and Glascock study locally produced coarse earthenware through compositional analysis. They examine how materials recovered in different contexts can inform the scale and organisation of production and exchange. Their conclusion challenges an initial assumption of only small, household-level production with intermittent distribution: the scale appeared larger than anticipated, while the authors do not claim a simple final answer to every route or production centre. That qualification is part of the finding. [2]

The method changes the question. Instead of beginning with a proprietor’s list of transactions, it starts with things used, broken, discarded and recovered. Similarities and differences in the materials can test whether a story of isolated plantation households is adequate. A ceramic sherd becomes evidence of technical practice and a possible connection. It does not give us the potter’s name, a complete trading itinerary or a photograph of the exchange.

The paper also relates pottery knowledge to women’s production and transmission, drawing on ethnographic evidence. This connects craft with social networks rather than treating a vessel as a timeless African survival. The analytical move is important: continuity must be investigated through practice, context and evidence. A shape that looks familiar does not automatically prove an uninterrupted tradition or identify a particular maker’s birthplace. [2]

An evidence chain, not a romantic reconstruction

  1. Object: locally made pottery recovered in a recorded archaeological context.
  2. Method: compare composition, manufacture and distribution.
  3. Inference: test possible production and exchange networks.
  4. Limit: do not invent an individual potter, sale or journey from a fragment.

The garden and the great house were in the same economy

Place the estate photograph beside the market photograph. One invites attention to an export-producing landscape; the other to circulation among many sellers and buyers. Neither can establish the eighteenth-century network on its own, but together they suggest why a history organised only around the great house is incomplete. Ask where people grew food, stored it, carried it, prepared it and sold it. Those tasks connect the landscape differently.

Emancipation did not create economic life from nothing

The National Library’s free-village account describes purchases of land and lots in the transition after emancipation. That settlement history should be read alongside the research on earlier production and exchange. The guide’s interpretation is that freed people were not blank economic subjects suddenly supplied with initiative. They entered a new legal situation with accumulated knowledge and relationships, while access to land and control of labour remained contested. [5]

Mintz and Hall’s 1960 study is an important entry in the research tradition on Jamaica’s internal markets; the author’s bibliography establishes that publication. It is offered as further reading here, not as an excuse to attach a famous name to claims from a book we have not independently inspected. The case made in this essay rests on the specific articles and evidence identified above. [4]

Hear the market as a problem, not just a tune

Louise Bennett’s Linstead Market performance supplies another way into the subject: a vendor’s account of disappointing trade is culturally meaningful even though the recording is later than the photographs and much later than slavery. The song is not a census or a financial return. Its value is to bring the work and uncertainty of selling into the listening session. Hear the identified performance in the guide → [6]

Put the evidence to work

Compare an estate-centred explanation with the pottery evidence. List one activity each source makes visible and one it leaves out. Explain how a provision ground could support a household and benefit an enslaver at the same time, without making those benefits equal.

Tony’s Call Put the food grower, trader and potter back into the economic story. Their knowledge mattered before emancipation, and recognising it should make the account of exploitation sharper.
