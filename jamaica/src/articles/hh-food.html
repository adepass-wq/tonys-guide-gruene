What Was Cooking in the Yard?

A household’s food history begins before the finished dish. Someone needed fuel, utensils and ingredients, and somewhere to prepare them. At Seville, those practical questions can be connected to excavated evidence. The result is more concrete than declaring a modern recipe an unchanged survival of an ancient cuisine.

The hearth was in the yard

House 15 had an outdoor hearth. Excavated material shows reused flint serving as fire starters, and locally made alongside imported ceramics. The researchers describe limited material means compared with managerial households and the repeated use of objects such as glass bottles. These are traces of obtaining, maintaining and adapting household equipment, rather than a surviving menu. [1]

A hearth establishes a place for fire-related activity; it does not establish what was cooked on every occasion. A vessel fragment identifies equipment more readily than a recipe. That makes the kitchen a history of work and resources as well as taste. The question is not simply whether people possessed objects, but how objects were used again when replacing them was difficult.

A familiar food with an unfamiliar journey

Breadfruit offers a documented example of why familiar Jamaican food should not be assigned a single origin story. A JIS account, drawing on its publication Our Culinary Heritage, describes its arrival from the South Pacific aboard William Bligh’s HMS Providence in 1793 and its use as inexpensive food for enslaved plantation workers. [2]

Arrival and belonging are different histories. A transported plant can become deeply embedded in Jamaican cooking without having originated in Jamaica or Africa. The useful distinction is between botanical movement, the colonial purpose of an introduction and the subsequent choices of people who prepared and ate it. The voyage date does not date every present-day breadfruit dish.

The market is part of the household story

Louise Bennett’s Jamaican Folk Songs recording includes market and work material, offering another way into the relationship between food and livelihood. [3]

Listen to Linstead Market as a performance about selling and its consequences, rather than as an audio reconstruction of Seville. Ask whose voice carries the story and how delivery makes the experience memorable. Then return to the excavated hearth: production, preparation and sale are connected, but they leave different kinds of evidence. Food becomes a richer subject when those differences remain visible.

The meal begins before the pot reaches the hearth

Food history joins obtaining ingredients, preparing a meal and deciding who eats. A vessel or hearth can document part of that chain, while market evidence brings exchange into view. Neither should be treated as a complete menu for every household. A single excavated object belongs to a context; the interpretation becomes stronger when several kinds of evidence agree.

Read household labor beside the banana economy. Growing a saleable crop and supplying a family's meals are connected demands on time and resources, but they are not identical activities. Export history can make production visible while leaving cooking, water carrying and care in the background. The domestic account restores those questions without assigning every task to an unnamed woman by assumption.

For a modern food stop, ask about the actual dish and your own needs rather than ordering a symbol of “authenticity.” The current meal is not an archaeological reconstruction. Its value is that it belongs to a living food culture whose ingredients, work and choices have histories. Read the Linstead market object for a second perspective on the labor between cultivation and consumption.

Tony’s Call Follow the meal back to the work that made it possible. A good food history can celebrate flavour while asking who controlled the land, equipment and value.
