Mento and Quadrille: Long before ska was the soundtrack of Jamaica

Mento tells stories about work, courtship, poverty, authority and everyday humor. Its acoustic sound often includes banjo, guitar, percussion and rhumba box. Quadrille, by contrast, reveals how European-derived social dances were transformed in Jamaican practice, with different styles incorporating local movement and musical interpretation. [2]

The first widely available commercial mento records date to the mid-twentieth century, but that does not mark the birth of the musical practices. We should distinguish evidence of recorded music from the longer, less fully documented history of live performance. Mento and Trinidadian calypso are related through Caribbean exchange, but they are not the same genre. [3]

The song tells a story; the band gives it a setting

Begin with the Jolly Boys recording, then return to Louise Bennett. One offers an ensemble performance associated with mento; the other foregrounds a singer and folklorist’s delivery of a market song. Compare the texture around the voice, the placement of repeated lines and the relationship between humor and rhythm. Those are audible observations a listener can make without assuming the recordings represent the entire history of either tradition.

A commercial record fixes one performance so it can circulate beyond its original audience. That gives the music a new life, but also encourages listeners to mistake the recorded arrangement for the definitive version. A song performed at a gathering can be longer, shorter, more interactive or differently accompanied. The question is not which version is more authentic in the abstract. It is who performed, for whom, with what equipment and under what circumstances.

Quadrille introduces another dimension: music coordinates a social arrangement of dancers. JCDC’s public categories distinguish ballroom, camp and contra styles. That distinction is a useful correction to the idea that all Jamaican quadrille follows one uniform pattern. A still image of a dance would not show the sequence; a film can, while still requiring identification of the particular style and group. The mento player here is not labeled as quadrille accompaniment. Read the lyric and social context together, but keep the identity of the performance intact. That is how listening becomes interpretation rather than background sound.

A song's social commentary survives a change of audience

Mento can be funny and historically serious at once. A lyric may organize a story, comment on conduct or make an audience recognize a situation. The band's sound supplies the setting in which that commentary becomes pleasurable to hear. Neither the humor nor the instrumentation should make the people and relationships disappear.

Quadrille supplies a different comparison: a named dance formation and its musical organization. Being placed beside mento on a heritage program does not make the two identical. Follow the particular recording or presentation for what it documents, retaining the performer and date.

The Jolly Boys material shows a named modern ensemble reaching an audience beyond an original local occasion. That circulation belongs to the story. Compare it with the older selections, asking what changes in arrangement, recording and public presentation. Then move to the listening room for ska, rocksteady and reggae without treating the older forms as traditions that stopped existing when a new genre arrived.

Tony’s Call: Pair lyrics with social context and annotate the humor. Songs that sound carefree can be sharply critical of economic inequality and relationships of power.
