St. James: enslaved labour, Sam Sharpe and the making of Montego Bay

Montego Bay’s airport, cruise port and resort coast grew over an older geography of slave trading, estate work, resistance, smallholdings and changing commerce.

A port that traded people as well as produce

Montego Bay’s wharves and merchants’ stores were sites of the disembarkation and sale of enslaved Africans. Jemmott uses newspaper advertisements to locate that commerce. This documentary evidence is stronger than treating an object called the “Slave Ring” as an unquestioned witness: the attribution of that structure is disputed. [1]

A place can preserve a compelling name while its exact function remains unsettled. The ads reveal a wider commercial network and the routine treatment of human beings as property. Port history therefore requires more than tracing cargo tonnage or admiring merchants’ buildings. It asks how commerce moved and controlled people.

The demand for paid work

Sam Sharpe, an enslaved Baptist deacon, organized through networks that included George Taylor, Thomas Dove and Robert Gardner. The demand concerned paid work after Christmas 1831. The Kensington fire on 27 December marked escalation into rebellion; repression included attacks on households and provision grounds. Sharpe was executed on 23 May 1832. [1] [2]

The organization matters as much as the most dramatic image of fire. Information, trust and meetings connected workers across estates. Destroying a house or provision ground during repression also attacked the means of sustaining life. The episode was a struggle over labour and authority, experienced in domestic space as well as in the fields.

Read the image as a representation

Duperly’s illustration of Roehampton’s destruction, published in 1833 and depicting January 1832, gives you a contemporary visual representation. It is not a photograph and it does not tell you every participant’s motive. Its composition makes the burning estate visible; registers, court records and other documents reveal different aspects of the rebellion.

The later Sam Sharpe memorial belongs to another historical moment: how the country remembers and honours resistance. The memorial photograph and the nineteenth-century print should be read together with their separate dates intact. One depicts an uprising through an artist’s frame; the other records public commemoration long afterward.

Emancipation and the interior

After 1838, freed people developed smallholdings in the interior while substantial coastal estate power persisted. Land was not redistributed wholesale. The geography of freedom included families obtaining ground, cultivating provisions and building institutions, alongside continuing commercial agriculture. [1]

This makes the parish’s hills important to understanding the resort coast. A road linking a village and a town can carry the history of a household’s access to markets, schooling and work. Do not assume the coastal estate tells the whole story or that every inland settlement followed one identical free-village pattern.

Banana ships, airport and Freeport

Late-nineteenth-century banana steamships also carried visitors. Montego Bay’s airport opened in 1947; Freeport’s reclamation of the Bogue mangrove islands was completed in 1968. The Free Zone began in 1985 with garment production and later diversified into other work. These changes linked transport, altered land and new employment. [1]

The modern arrival experience is therefore historical infrastructure. A runway, deepwater port and industrial zone concentrate particular flows of people and goods. Tourism grew through those connections, while service work and production changed form. A visitor’s short transfer from the airport travels through choices that reshaped the parish over decades.

Rose Hall: put the documents ahead of the witch

The White Witch narrative draws on literary and tourism folklore. Zacek’s archival scholarship distinguishes the historical women whose identities became entangled in the legend. Celia Naylor instead follows enslaved women, men and children through registers and the Rose Hall Journal. Names, approximate ages, births, deaths and escapes restore people whom a ghost-centred tour can leave unnamed. [3] [5]

Naylor’s companion project documents 208 enslaved people. Its entries are evidence-led attempts to reconstruct lives from incomplete records; they are not permission to invent dialogue or pretend that a register contains a full biography. The historical force of Rose Hall lies in the people and plantation system, not the supposed supernatural powers of one owner. [4]

Present visits belong to a continuing history

Melissa’s impacts on Rose Hall and Greenwood appear in the national damage assessment. That establishes documented damage; it does not establish present admission or completed restoration. The great-house portal separately checks access, with Greenwood currently closed. [6]

Explore the square and a named heritage programme in daylight, arrange interiors through their operators and give the hills room in your understanding of the parish. St. James becomes more legible when the port, estate, village and present employment landscape remain connected.
