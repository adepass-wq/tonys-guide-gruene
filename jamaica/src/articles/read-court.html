St. Ann’s slave court: the plantation economy on trial, through the colony’s own pen

A surviving court book brings pimento, livestock, escape and testimony into the history of St. Ann. It shows how everyday disputes entered a legal system controlled by people with power over the accused.

A valuable crop became an accusation

The National Library of Jamaica identifies a case concerning pimento taken from Mount Pleasant Plantation in a court book whose first entry is dated April 9, 1787, and whose last is April 12, 1814. Pimento is a useful starting point because it connects the familiar parish landscape to labor, ownership and law. A crop could be represented as plantation income in one account and alleged theft in another. The criminal charge did not stand apart from the economic system; it helped enforce that system’s claims. [1]

The catalogue also identifies proceedings involving the killing of hogs, murder and escape from enslavement. These are different alleged acts, and their meanings cannot be reduced to one motive. What unites them in this collection is the institutional setting: the people accused were judged in a slave society. An act of flight challenged an owner’s claim over a person. A dispute over food or animals implicated the resources through which an estate and its residents lived. [1]

The parish connected property to public authority

The court was presided over by white justices of the peace, including merchants and planters. The Library describes severe sentences and records of rulings as well as affidavits and proceedings. People linked to the commercial and agricultural order participated in judging people subordinated within it. This was not an equal dispute between independent citizens whose only difference was the particular charge. [1]

Other records make that connection visible. The 1817 almanac’s St. Ann returns list proprietors, properties, enslaved people and livestock, and state that the information was supplied to parish vestries for the March 1816 quarter. Property administration and local government overlapped. Those tables do not identify the outcome of the Mount Pleasant case, but they describe the administrative world surrounding the court: owners and holdings were visible, while the people held in slavery could be reduced to a count. [2]

The testimony is important because the record is unequal

The court book preserves testimony attributed to enslaved people. That is its human importance. Someone who appears only as a number in a property return can become a speaker, witness or accused person in a proceeding. Yet the written words were selected and recorded by others. Questioning, translation, summary and the expectations of officials shaped what reached the page. The Library explicitly identifies this tension between the voices preserved and the biases of those preserving them. [1]

That does not make the source worthless. It changes how we read it. A deposition can establish what a clerk recorded someone as saying without demonstrating that the record contains every word, every circumstance or an entirely voluntary statement. A judgment can show what the court decided without making its account a neutral explanation of the event. The record gives access to both a fragment of a person’s experience and the institution that framed it.

The surviving book has its own history

DOCUMENTARY SOURCE · ST. ANN · 1787–1814

The court record behind this article

The National Library of Jamaica identifies the Record Book of the Court of St. Ann as MS 273. Its catalogue description is a route to the surviving source, rather than a facsimile or a transcript of a named trial.

Read the National Library’s catalogue description ↗

St. Ann’s Parochial Board presented the book to the Institute of Jamaica in 1922. The National Library microfilmed it in 1985. Its catalogue records 383 microfilm pages, pale text, water damage and at least six missing pages. The original’s condition has restricted ordinary handling. These physical details explain why silence in the surviving record is not proof that an event or a person never existed. [1]

The catalogue is not a published transcript of every trial. This article consequently does not invent a defendant’s name, speech, punishment or motive for the pimento case. The established history is already consequential: a court in St. Ann heard accusations arising from plantation life, recorded enslaved testimony and operated through officials connected to the slaveholding economy. A fictional courtroom scene would make the article more dramatic while making the history less reliable.

From a resort parish to a parish of ordinary lives

The fishing-boat photograph identifies St. Ann’s Bay in 2023. It is regional context rather than a view of Mount Pleasant or the court. It reminds a visitor that the parish contains working communities, institutions and histories extending beyond attractions. The court book belongs beside the story of free villages and the family records: one exposes coercive legal authority, another describes efforts to secure land after emancipation, and a third recovers people obscured by property administration.

The result is a different account of heritage. Great houses document an owner’s position; legal and household records reveal the lives required to sustain that position. St. Ann’s history is richer when a crop, an accusation and an attributed voice are allowed to stand beside the monumental building.

A case file is an encounter with unequal power

The court book records more than a list of incidents. It shows how an institution selected names, classified acts and preserved testimony. Pimento, livestock and movement become visible because they entered a dispute or prosecution. The fact that an activity appears in a legal record does not tell us how common it was outside the courtroom.

Ask whose words reach us directly, whose statement is summarized and whose position is described by someone with authority. A precise quotation can still be an unequal account. Reading against that imbalance does not permit invention; it directs us toward other records that might identify relationships, circumstances or outcomes.

Pair this article with Port Maria's courthouse and Morant Bay. The comparison follows legal authority across different dates and settings. It does not make every case a rebellion or every building an identical institution. The deeper historical question is how ordinary livelihood and movement became matters over which people with power could judge and punish others.

Tony’s Call Use the court book to expose the system and recover people within it. Do not turn a catalogue description into an invented trial.
