Morant Bay: an inquiry could condemn brutality and narrow justice.

Paul Bogle’s rebellion challenged land, taxation, representation and colonial power. Jonathan Connolly’s research shows how the official inquiry and legal controversy redirected that larger argument into questions about the governor’s authority.

The claim under examination

“The inquiry into Morant Bay simply established the truth and restored impartial British justice.”

What the evidence changes: Inquiry produced important evidence and criticism, but also shaped what counted as relevant. Connolly argues that legal proceedings displaced a systemic challenge to colonial rule and helped legitimate more centralised imperial control.

Begin before the courthouse burned

On 11 October 1865, protesters approached the courthouse at Morant Bay. Connolly places the attempted arrest of Bogle within a wider history of poverty, taxation, restricted representation and a legal system dominated by planter interests. The event did become violent. But reducing it to a spontaneous eruption of disorder discards the political claims made before and around that violence. A crowd has a history, not merely a headcount. [1]

The “Underhill meetings,” petitions and public arguments discussed in the article are important because they show Jamaicans interpreting their own situation. Their complaints concerned how colonial policy constrained life after slavery. The issue was not simply whether a benevolent authority would hear a request more kindly. It was whether freedom could have substance when land, taxation and political power remained arranged against the labouring population.

The Queen’s advice was not an economically neutral answer

Connolly examines a dispatch known as the “Queen’s Advice,” drafted by the Colonial Office and circulated by Governor Eyre. It rejected the assessment of deep colonial poverty and emphasized wage labour. Protesters’ responses questioned the reality of post-slavery freedom. The apparent disagreement over diligence was also a disagreement over the structure of opportunities. Who controlled land? Who bore taxes? Who decided that the worker’s circumstances were the worker’s own fault? [1]

Keep the questions visible

The protesters’ larger questions: land, wages, taxation, representation and the meaning of freedom.

The later legal focus: martial law, the governor’s authority and individual criminal responsibility.

These are the guide’s summary of Connolly’s argument, not a facsimile of Bogle’s petition or the commission’s report.

This is a useful surprise because it changes the starting point of the history. If we begin with the burning courthouse, we are likely to ask only about the restoration of order. If we begin with the preceding petitions and arguments, we can see why the existing order was itself under challenge. That does not excuse every act of the rebellion. It establishes the problem that participants were attempting to address.

Suppression and investigation were different exercises of power

The suppression continued under martial law after organised resistance had quickly been put down. Connolly describes killings, flogging and destruction of homes and shows how the royal commission condemned major aspects of that conduct. An inquiry that records and criticizes abuse has real evidentiary value. The harder question is what it makes visible while setting other questions aside. [1]

He argues that the commission and later proceedings translated a broad critique of colonial society into a narrower dispute over the proper use of executive power. That is an interpretation advanced through detailed documentary analysis, not the only possible definition of the event. It complements earlier scholarship on the rebellion and on British debates over race and martial law. We should state the argument as his, then examine whether the records support it.

An important campaign could still leave the empire intact

John Stuart Mill and the Jamaica Committee sought to hold Eyre accountable. Their challenge to arbitrary punishment was consequential; it should not be dismissed as empty theatre. Connolly nonetheless shows how the language of protected imperial subjects could preserve the larger imperial framework. Defending people against abuse and questioning the legitimacy of colonial rule were related projects, but they were not identical. [1]

The contrast is especially clear in the proceedings against Eyre. Questions of criminal responsibility concerned identifiable actions and lawful authority, not the entire economic organisation that had produced unrest. Legal relevance narrowed the available field. A court could ask whether a governor had exceeded his powers without deciding whether the political economy he administered was unjust. The limits could operate through legal categories even without every participant deliberately trying to silence a grievance.

What the failed prosecution establishes

The Middlesex grand jury rejected the indictment in 1868. It is therefore inaccurate to tell this episode as a completed trial and conviction of Eyre. Connolly analyzes Justice Blackburn’s charge as the culminating legal pronouncement because the case did not proceed as the campaigners hoped. Procedural precision matters: an attempted prosecution, instructions to a jury and a conviction are different events with different implications. [1]

The essay’s conclusion is more unsettling than “Britain corrected a rogue governor.” Investigating the scandal could reposition the problem as poor local governance and support further centralisation of imperial control. In that reading, the system absorbed a challenge by redefining it. The historian’s task is not simply to repeat the commission’s verdict, but to investigate how the verdict altered the story being told.

A hero’s image should lead us back to the claims

Bogle’s place as a national hero preserves the importance of his leadership and sacrifice. JIS and JNHT provide routes into that public memory and the surviving courthouse site. The danger comes when a memorial encounter stops at an image and an execution date. A more informed visit asks what people wanted, how they organised, and what the later official narrative did with those demands. [4]

The earlier history linked below supplies local context; this piece adds a particular scholarly argument about inquiry. Reading the two together helps prevent a new simplification. Connolly’s interpretation does not mean every witness was unheard or every British campaigner insincere. It means that producing an official account is itself a historical act with rules, priorities and consequences.

Put the evidence to work

Make two lists: grievances before the uprising, and questions addressed in the Eyre proceedings. Identify a grievance that does not fit easily into a prosecution for misuse of martial law. Explain why condemning unlawful punishment could leave that grievance unresolved.

Tony’s Call At Morant Bay, read the demands as carefully as the punishment. An inquiry can expose brutality while changing the question the public is invited to ask.
