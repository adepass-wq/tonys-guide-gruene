The Jamaican diaspora did not begin at Tilbury.

Panama and the wider Caribbean basin restore a migration history that a Windrush-only narrative compresses. Work records reveal both movement and unequal treatment.

← The second runRead in sequence →

The account under examination

1948 supplies the starting point for Jamaica’s modern history of people building lives overseas.

What the evidence changes: Windrush is a pivotal British episode within a much older and wider movement. Jamaican and other Caribbean migrants crossed regional borders, worked, formed families and encountered racial barriers before that arrival.

A ship can become too large a symbol

The Empire Windrush’s 1948 arrival gives Britain a powerful commemorative scene. It does not supply the beginning of every Caribbean migration history. Our Windrush article retains that particular voyage, passenger evidence and newsreel interviews; this essay changes the geographical frame around it rather than diminishing the event.

Lara Putnam’s Radical Moves examines African-descended working-class migration from the British Caribbean into Panama, Cuba, Venezuela and North America after emancipation, alongside the barriers that developed in the interwar decades. Her account concerns a regional population; we must not relabel every migrant in it as Jamaican. [1]

The canal was also a place people lived

US National Archives guidance identifies Jamaica among the islands from which West Indian workers came to the Canal Zone. It points to labour contracts, sailing lists and employment records, and also to marriage, birth and death records. The archive therefore offers a route into families as well as construction labour. [2]

An engineering achievement invites a story of excavation, locks and ships. A household history asks different questions: who travelled together, who followed later and where children were recorded. These need named records. We should not invent a representative Jamaican family and disguise it as history merely because the general circumstances are well documented.

Opportunity came with unequal terms

The archivist’s account describes racial discrimination in jobs, wages, housing and working conditions. It also explains that surviving West Indian employment records are less extensive than the white employee records. Archival unevenness is part of the research problem, not a measure of which workers mattered more. [2]

This changes how absence should be interpreted. Failure to locate one person in an accessible series cannot establish that the person never worked there. Before drawing that conclusion, check the series’ coverage, spelling variants, jurisdiction and the possibility of commuting from outside the Canal Zone. A database’s empty result is a finding about that search, not a verdict on a life.

Gold and silver became a racial geography

The Canal Zone’s gold and silver payrolls were more than methods of payment. Patrice Brown’s archival account shows that their classifications organized housing, schools, recreation and access to services as well as employment benefits. Skill and citizenship were invoked as explanations, but the records reveal racial discrimination operating through the system. African American employees’ cases also expose its complications: citizenship, pay currency and roll membership did not always line up. This is a regional history of unequal treatment, not a claim that every Black worker had an identical employment status. [4]

In the photograph of workers’ quarters, housing therefore belongs to the labour story. A move abroad might offer earnings while placing a family within another hierarchy of rights and facilities. Migration was neither a universal escape from colonial inequality nor proof that opportunities did not exist. Establish both the job and the conditions attached to it before calling a particular family’s journey a success or failure.

A photograph should not assign nationality

Wellcome’s image of workers’ quarters at Cristobal, around 1910, places children and wooden dwellings in a landscape of segregated accommodation. Its catalogue identifies West Indian and Panamanian workers; it does not establish that the pictured children or every resident were Jamaican. The scene can support a discussion of housing while leaving those identities open. [3]

The distinction is more than a caption technicality. A picture circulated as “Jamaicans building Panama” can quietly turn a multi-island and Panamanian history into an exclusive national claim. Jamaica belongs in that history through the evidence; it does not need ownership of every face in the photograph.

Movement could carry political ideas

Putnam connects migrant networks and popular culture with Black internationalism and anticolonial politics. That interpretation asks us to follow communication and affiliation alongside employment. A migration account made only of departure dates and destinations misses what people did with the relationships movement made possible. [1]

The revised starting point is therefore a network, not a single ship. Panama is one entry into it, and Windrush is another. Recover a person’s route through the records rather than assuming it followed a later commemorative timeline. The diaspora becomes both older and less geographically predictable when we stop making Britain its only destination.
